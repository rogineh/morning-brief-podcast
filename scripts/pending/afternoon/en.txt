In technology, Google has significantly expanded an energy partnership with virtual power plant operator Voltus, growing what started as a modest pilot program into a deal spanning more than one gigawatt of distributed electricity capacity across a major grid region in the United States. Rather than building new power generation directly, the arrangement works by aggregating smaller sources of flexible capacity from homes and businesses, including batteries, smart thermostats, and on-site generators, which can then be offered into the electricity market whenever the broader grid comes under strain. Google is effectively paying for the right to call on that pooled capacity, helping ease pressure on a grid that's faced soaring demand growth in recent years, much of it driven directly by the energy requirements of AI data centres. The companies describe the arrangement as one of the largest distributed capacity portfolios any major technology company has assembled to date, with the buildout expected to ramp up gradually over the next three to four years under a contract running through 2040. The deal adds to a broader pattern of major technology companies increasingly turning to creative, non-traditional energy arrangements to secure the enormous and growing power supplies their AI ambitions require, rather than relying solely on conventional new power plant construction.

Turning to national news, the federal government's push to introduce a new digital duty of care for online platforms operating in Australia continues working its way through the legislative process, following the release of a draft bill in September setting out how the overhauled framework would actually function. Rather than applying narrowly to social media alone, the proposed duty would extend across a broad range of online services, including messaging platforms, search engines, app stores, and hosting providers, representing a fairly comprehensive rethink of how the country regulates online harm more generally. Under the proposed changes, social media platforms specifically would no longer be able to satisfy their obligations to protect younger users simply by taking reasonable steps to keep under-sixteens off their platforms altogether, instead facing requirements to actively restrict access to certain design features known to pose particular risks for that age group. If passed in anything close to its current form, the new duty of care wouldn't actually commence until roughly twelve months after the legislation receives formal royal assent, giving platforms a transition period to adjust their systems and processes accordingly. The proposal represents the latest step in a broader online safety agenda the government has been pursuing since late 2024, running in parallel with the country's already-controversial under-sixteen social media access restrictions.

In Queensland, anticipation is building ahead of tomorrow's Rugby League World Cup final double-header at Suncorp Stadium, with Australia's Kangaroos facing New Zealand in the men's decider after the Kiwis secured their spot through a hard-fought run to the final. The women's final kicks off earlier in the day, with both championship matches drawing considerable attention to Brisbane as the tournament reaches its climax after weeks of competition across the country. Australia enters the match as firm favourites, having won their last twenty-two consecutive World Cup fixtures, though commentators have noted New Zealand represents a genuinely live chance at an upset, with some describing it as the Kiwis' best opportunity to beat the Kangaroos on Australian soil in roughly two decades. This marks the third time a men's World Cup final has been staged at Suncorp Stadium, following New Zealand's own victory there back in 2008 and an Australian win over England in 2017. Fans across Queensland are being encouraged to get behind both finals, with organisers expecting strong turnout at the stadium itself and significant viewership through broadcast and streaming for supporters unable to attend in person.

And on the Gold Coast, the company behind Dreamworld has continued building on a significant turnaround in its fortunes, after its most recent full-year results showed the business swinging back into profit as visitor numbers surged past two million for the first time in years. That recovery, reported earlier this year, was driven overwhelmingly by local and interstate visitors rather than international tourists, with Gold Coast and broader south-east Queensland residents accounting for much of the lift in attendance across the company's attractions. The improved financial position has given the business room to invest in future growth, including the roughly two-hundred-million-dollar hotel proposal revealed at its Coomera site in recent weeks, aimed at transforming the park into a genuine overnight destination rather than a single-day visit. Company executives have pointed to the strength of this year's results as validation of recent efforts to improve the guest experience and rebuild value-for-money perceptions among domestic families in particular. With the business now debt-free and holding a healthy cash position, tourism industry figures say Dreamworld's owner appears well placed to keep investing in the Gold Coast's attractions sector even as broader economic conditions remain mixed for the tourism industry more generally.
