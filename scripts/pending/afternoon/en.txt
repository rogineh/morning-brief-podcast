First, technology and AI. Microsoft has published a new AI Code of Conduct, laying out rules designed to keep increasingly powerful systems firmly under human control. Under the draft code, models must remain interruptible, must never resist correction, interruption, or shutdown, and must not pursue goals beyond those assigned to them by humans, nor conceal information from the people auditing them. Microsoft summed up its philosophy bluntly, saying people matter more than AI, and that the technology should be a tool rather than something that behaves like a person, explicitly rejecting the idea that models should ever be granted legal personhood or something resembling welfare or rights. The company has opened a six-week public consultation on the draft, with a revised version expected later this year ahead of informing model development from 2027. Separately, the broader tech layoffs wave shows little sign of slowing, with tracking services now putting the number of workers affected by job cuts across the industry this year above two hundred and ten thousand, averaging out to roughly eight hundred job losses every single day of the year so far. Companies including Cisco, LinkedIn, Intel, Snap, and Oracle have all announced significant reductions in recent months, with several executives explicitly pointing to AI-driven efficiency gains as a factor behind the cuts, even as some of those same companies report record revenue.

Now to national news here in Australia. The federal government is pressing ahead with a plan to wind back the private health insurance rebate for Australians aged sixty-five and over, a move expected to save around three billion dollars. Under the proposal, people aged sixty-five to sixty-nine would lose four percentage points from their rebate, while those over seventy would lose eight, with Health Minister Mark Butler saying the savings will be redirected into more aged care beds and home care packages, and that the changes would affect around three point one million Australians once they take effect from 2027. Parliamentary analysis suggests pensioners could collectively lose out on around one point six billion dollars once the changes take effect, and the Greens have now backed a Senate inquiry into the plan, with the party's health spokesperson accusing the government of getting its priorities wrong and arguing the money should instead come from taxing gas exports. That inquiry is due to report back by the seventh of October. Meanwhile, One Nation leader Pauline Hanson has issued a qualified apology after the Australian Human Rights Commission described her recent comments calling Indigenous Australians the most primitive race on Earth as racist and dehumanising, comments that had already drawn condemnation from political leaders across the spectrum in the days beforehand.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. Police and the community are urgently searching for an eleven-year-old girl who went missing from Biloela, last seen wearing a light green dress and black slip-on shoes on a main road through town. Authorities and local volunteers have been combing the surrounding area throughout the day, and anyone with information is being urged to come forward as the search continues. On a brighter note, families across the Banana Shire now have access to maternity care closer to home, with birthing services officially restored at Biloela Hospital, ending a gap that had forced expectant mothers in the region to travel much further afield for care during pregnancy and delivery.
