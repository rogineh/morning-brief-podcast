In technology, United Airlines has moved to block unauthorised third-party AI agents from booking, servicing, or purchasing flights through its website, app, and other digital sales channels, citing privacy, security, and regulatory concerns. The airline says personal AI assistants will still be able to search for fares and display flight options on a customer's behalf, but the actual purchase step now has to go through a channel United has authorised itself, rather than an independent AI tool acting on a traveller's instructions. The company has stressed that existing bookings made through AI agents before the policy took effect won't be cancelled retroactively, and it's described the move as largely precautionary given how few customers have actually attempted AI-agent bookings to date. The decision reflects a broader tension playing out across the travel industry, as airlines weigh the convenience AI assistants offer travellers against concerns about fraud, data privacy, and compliance with aviation and consumer protection regulations that weren't designed with autonomous software agents in mind. Other airlines and major travel platforms are widely expected to watch how this policy plays out, with some industry commentators predicting more carriers will introduce similar restrictions as AI-driven booking tools become more capable and more widely used by everyday travellers.

Turning to national news, Australia's big four banks have wrapped up a mixed full-year reporting season, with combined profits across the sector rising to just over thirty-one billion dollars, up close to five per cent on the previous year. ANZ emerged as the standout performer, posting a twenty-three per cent jump in annual profit, while Westpac bucked the broader trend of improved earnings, recording its first annual profit decline in years as its bottom line slipped slightly compared with the prior financial year. Analysts have pointed to improved cost discipline and steady lending growth as key drivers behind the sector's overall improved profitability, even as banks continue flagging credit risk and softer revenue growth as ongoing concerns heading into the new financial year. The reporting season wrapped up just as the Reserve Bank's cash rate settled at four point eight five per cent following its most recent increase earlier this month, a tightening in financial conditions that occurred after most of the banks' reporting periods had already closed. Economists say the combination of resilient bank profitability and a higher cash rate leaves households facing continued pressure on mortgage repayments, even as the banking sector itself appears to be weathering the current economic conditions reasonably comfortably compared with some other parts of the economy.

In Queensland, work continues progressing on the planning and early construction phases of Brisbane's new Olympic stadium at Victoria Park, with civil contractor BMD continuing early site works ahead of the main construction phase expected to begin in 2027. Two major multinational construction partners remain in the running to deliver the main build itself, currently working through an early contractor involvement process designed to test the practical buildability of the stadium's ambitious sixty-three-thousand-seat design before a final builder is locked in later this year. The venue, budgeted at roughly three point six billion dollars, will become the centrepiece stadium for the 2032 Brisbane Olympic and Paralympic Games, eventually replacing the Gabba as the city's primary major sporting venue once the new stadium opens. Project authorities say the staged approach of appointing an early works contractor well ahead of the main builder reflects lessons learned from other major Olympic infrastructure projects internationally, where early site preparation and design testing have proven critical to keeping enormous venues like this one on schedule and within budget. With the final builder decision still pending, attention across the construction industry remains firmly fixed on which of the two shortlisted partners will ultimately be awarded what's shaping up as one of the largest single infrastructure contracts in the lead-up to the Games.

And on the Gold Coast, construction has begun on Kaiya Kirra Beach, a boutique medium-rise apartment development on the southern Gold Coast that's been in the planning pipeline for several years after going through multiple design revisions. The project, valued at around a hundred and thirty million dollars, will bring a mix of two, three, and four-bedroom apartments to the site, along with a small number of ground-level beach houses featuring their own private garaging, with completion currently targeted for late 2028. The development's long and winding path through the planning process, including earlier taller designs that were ultimately scaled back to comply with local height restrictions, reflects a broader pattern playing out across several beachside Gold Coast suburbs, where developers continue testing the limits of what councils and communities are willing to approve for low-rise coastal precincts. Local real estate figures say demand for boutique, lower-density beachside apartments has remained strong even as larger high-rise towers dominate headlines elsewhere along the coast, with buyers increasingly drawn to smaller developments that preserve more of the laid-back, village-style character long associated with suburbs like Kirra. With construction now under way, the project adds to a steady pipeline of medium-scale residential developments continuing to reshape the southern end of the Gold Coast's coastal strip.
