In technology, financial markets are bracing for a pivotal stretch of earnings next week from the major cloud and AI players, in results widely seen as a real test of whether the industry's enormous spending on AI infrastructure is translating into genuine returns. Microsoft's most recent results put its cloud revenue at fifty nine point three billion dollars, up twenty seven per cent on the year, with its Microsoft 365 Copilot assistant now passing thirty million paid seats, while Alphabet has reported Google Cloud revenue of twenty four point eight billion dollars, up a striking eighty two per cent, alongside a healthy operating margin. Meta is also due to report, with analysts expecting its capital spending to come in near forty one billion dollars for the quarter, up around one hundred and seventeen per cent on the same period last year, almost entirely driven by continued investment in AI data centres and computing capacity. Investors say the key question hanging over all of these results is whether rising depreciation costs and the sheer scale of accelerator purchases will start to squeeze profit margins, even as revenue growth from AI products and services continues to look strong on paper. With roughly a third of the S&P 500 reporting results in the same stretch, alongside a US Federal Reserve meeting midweek, markets are expecting a genuinely volatile few days ahead as investors try to work out whether the AI trade still has room to run.

Turning to national news, a tsunami warning issued for parts of the Pacific following two large earthquakes off the coast of Vanuatu earlier today has since been lifted, with the Bureau of Meteorology confirming no threat was ever issued for the Australian coastline. The first earthquake, measured at magnitude six point nine, struck around midday Australian eastern time, followed shortly after by a second quake measured at six point three, prompting the Pacific Tsunami Warning Centre to flag the possibility of hazardous waves for coastlines within around three hundred kilometres of the epicentre. That warning was rescinded within a few hours once monitoring agencies were able to confirm the risk had passed, and no damage or casualties have so far been reported from the earthquakes themselves. The brief scare serves as a reminder of the region's ongoing seismic activity, with Vanuatu sitting on a particularly active stretch of the Pacific Ring of Fire that regularly produces earthquakes of this scale.

In Queensland, the state's child safety and domestic violence minister, Amanda Camm, has stepped down from cabinet, in what's being described as the first frontbench change since the Crisafulli government came to power back in 2024. In a statement, Camm said the decision was deeply personal and that her own health and wellbeing needed to take priority, adding that she alone made the call and informed the Premier only the day before announcing it publicly. The move follows a stretch of sustained political pressure over recent months, including heavy criticism of a troubled rollout of a new child safety IT system, reported to have cost around one hundred and eighty three million dollars, which left frontline officers temporarily unable to access vital case information including existing court orders for at-risk children. Camm had also faced criticism after state budget papers revealed funding for domestic violence services had been cut by close to forty million dollars, prompting repeated opposition calls for her removal from the portfolio. The Premier said Camm had been handed an enormous task, overseeing a child safety system he described as being in crisis alongside the need for broader domestic violence reform, and said he had not pushed for her resignation, with a wider ministerial reshuffle now expected in the coming days as a replacement is named.

And on the Gold Coast, city councillors have voted to delay planned three-hour parking restrictions along streets between Mermaid Beach and Burleigh Heads, pushing the changes back until traffic monitoring surveys can be carried out in April and again in October of next year. The restrictions, tied to the opening of the light rail's third stage, had already been pushed back once after signage wasn't ready in time, and faced a petition with more than two thousand signatures calling for a much longer delay while real traffic patterns in the area were properly assessed. Councillors who pushed for the further delay argued that locking in new parking limits without first gathering solid local data risked getting the balance wrong between protecting resident access and keeping the new light rail corridor moving freely. Local business owners along the affected strip, who had previously said they simply wanted certainty either way, will now need to wait considerably longer than first expected for a final resolution on exactly when, or in what form, the new parking limits will eventually take effect.
