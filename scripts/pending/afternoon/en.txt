In technology, cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike has launched what it's calling an AI kill switch, a new feature designed to let security teams instantly shut down internal artificial intelligence agents that start behaving unexpectedly or operating outside their intended bounds. The new capability forms part of the company's broader platform for securing AI agents, and allows security staff to stop a rogue agent in its tracks, block it from automatically relaunching, and cut off its network access entirely once a problem has been identified. Rather than deleting an agent's underlying code outright, the system works by cutting the agent's access to the AI models actually driving its decisions, severing the connection between the agent and the intelligence powering it while leaving a complete audit trail of every action taken. At launch, the kill switch functionality operates specifically at the gateway level connecting agents to AI models, with the company planning to extend direct termination capabilities on individual devices and endpoints in a future release down the track. The announcement came alongside news that CrowdStrike has agreed to acquire two smaller AI monitoring and security companies for close to five hundred million dollars combined, reflecting the wider cybersecurity industry's growing focus on managing the genuine risks posed by autonomous AI systems operating with real decision-making power inside corporate networks.

Turning to national news, Australia is preparing to formally lead global climate negotiations when the United Nations' major annual climate summit opens in Turkey this coming Monday, continuing close to two weeks of talks involving governments from around the world. Australia holds the specific title of President of Negotiations for this year's summit, working directly alongside Turkey as the official host nation to help broker outcomes across what's expected to be a genuinely challenging set of discussions. The federal Climate Change and Energy Minister will personally take on this negotiating leadership role throughout the summit, representing a significant diplomatic responsibility for Australia on the global stage regardless of the specific position the country stakes out on any individual contested issue. A dedicated World Leaders' Summit segment is scheduled for the middle of next week, when heads of state and government from around the world are expected to gather in person to set the broader political tone for the remainder of the technical negotiations that follow. The role gives Australia considerable influence over how discussions are structured and which issues get prioritised during the fortnight of talks, even though final decisions on any agreed outcomes will ultimately require consensus among all participating nations rather than resting with Australia alone.

In Queensland, new figures released today show the number of victims of serious offences committed by people already on bail has fallen by close to ten per cent since the state government's broader community safety plan came into effect. The data comes as authorities continue preparing for a fresh round of bail law changes due to take effect toward the end of this month, which will introduce a minimum twelve-month prison sentence for anyone who commits a serious offence while already out on bail. Under the strengthened test being introduced alongside that minimum sentence, courts will need to be satisfied to a considerably higher standard that an accused person genuinely won't reoffend before granting bail on serious charges going forward. The government has pointed to today's victim number figures as early evidence its broader approach to community safety is already delivering meaningful results, even ahead of the toughest elements of the reformed bail laws actually taking legal effect later this month. Legal groups and some advocates have previously raised concerns about the practical impact of the changes on an already strained court and prison system, a debate that's likely to continue as the new rules move closer to formally taking hold.

And on the Gold Coast, hundreds of outrigger canoe paddlers have converged on the city as competition gets underway today in one of the standout events at this year's Pan Pacific Masters Games, with more than three hundred and fifty athletes taking part across a dedicated two-day competition. The paddling events form just one small part of the broader ten-day masters sporting festival now properly underway across the city, with tonight's official opening ceremony at the Gold Coast Convention and Exhibition Centre in Broadbeach set to formally mark the occasion. Competitors in the outrigger events range widely in age and experience, reflecting the broader masters games philosophy of providing genuinely competitive, officiated sport for athletes well beyond their peak competitive years rather than limiting serious competition to younger age groups alone. Local tourism operators say events like the outrigger competition, which draw entire paddling clubs and their extended support networks to the city for several days at a time, are proving particularly valuable for generating sustained visitor spending throughout the games rather than just a single day's economic activity. With competition continuing across dozens of different sports through until the games wrap up with a closing ceremony on the fourteenth of November, organisers say today's strong turnout across individual events like outrigger canoeing reflects genuinely successful planning for what's shaping up as one of the city's biggest sporting festivals in recent years.
