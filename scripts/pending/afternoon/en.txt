In technology, Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has floated the idea of charging roughly two thousand dollars per graphics processing unit each year for software that runs AI agents across what the company calls AI factories, signalling a significant shift in how Nvidia plans to make money beyond simply selling hardware. Huang raised the figure during a presentation in Berlin last week, describing software licensing as set to become an increasingly major part of the company's overall business, though Nvidia has stopped short of confirming it as an actual, formal price. The comment has prompted plenty of speculation, given the company already charges more than twice that amount, around four and a half thousand dollars per GPU annually, for its existing AI Enterprise software suite, leaving analysts unsure whether the lower figure represents a narrower bundle, a future pricing change, or simply an illustrative example Huang used on the day. Commentators have nonetheless run the numbers on what such a licensing model could mean at scale, with some suggesting it could eventually become a multi-billion dollar annual revenue stream in its own right if enough customers signed on and kept renewing. The episode highlights how central software and recurring revenue have become to Nvidia's long-term strategy, even as the company continues to dominate the market for the physical chips that underpin the broader AI boom.

Turning to national news, Foodbank Australia's latest annual hunger report has found that three point six million Australian households experienced food insecurity over the past year, an increase of around one hundred thousand households compared with the previous report. The findings show roughly one in three households nationally experienced some form of food insecurity, with close to seventy per cent of those affected households saying they were struggling to access enough food for the first time, suggesting the problem is reaching well beyond those already considered most vulnerable. Households with children have been particularly affected, with close to forty five per cent of such households reporting food insecurity, and single parent households facing an even higher rate of around sixty per cent. Perhaps most strikingly, more than a third of middle income households, earning broadly between thirty four and ninety one thousand dollars a year, reported food insecurity, pointing to just how far cost of living pressures have spread up the income scale. Foodbank's chief executive linked the rise directly to the ongoing cost of living crisis and elevated interest rates, while noting that participation in school breakfast programs among affected families has climbed noticeably over the past year as households look for whatever support they can access.

In Queensland, a draft water plan covering the remote Diamantina and Georgina river catchments in the state's far west has alarmed graziers and producers downstream near Birdsville, who fear changes to overland flow rules could meaningfully reduce water reaching properties further down the system. The most significant change in the draft plan would double the amount of identified unallocated water in the catchment from thirteen and a half thousand megalitres to twenty seven thousand megalitres, alongside simplified rules for managing groundwater that's connected to the rivers themselves. One prominent organic beef producer in the region has warned the changes could affect flows in the slow-moving rivers of the Lake Eyre Basin, potentially cutting into the income of cattle operations that depend on natural flooding to replenish pasture across vast grazing areas. The state government says it genuinely wants input from local communities and affected stakeholders before finalising the plan, with in-person consultation sessions being held in coming days and formal submissions remaining open until the middle of December. The existing water plan for the region dates back to 2004 and is due to expire in August of next year, meaning a replacement will need to be finalised one way or another well before then.

And on the Gold Coast, the city marked a new transport milestone yesterday with the arrival of the first commercial flight from the newly opened Western Sydney International Airport, after Jetstar's inaugural service touched down following a journey of a little over an hour. The flight carried a mix of media, VIP guests, and paying passengers, with the airline reporting that tickets for the historic first service sold out within forty five minutes of going on sale. Jetstar has flagged plans to run the Gold Coast route on a daily basis given stronger than expected early demand, a step up from the four weekly services the airline had originally pencilled in before the airport's opening. The new direct link gives Gold Coast residents and visitors another option for reaching Sydney's fast-growing western suburbs without needing to fly into the more congested Kingsford Smith Airport on the other side of the harbour city. Local tourism operators have welcomed the new connection, saying additional direct routes into the Gold Coast from any major population centre tend to translate fairly directly into extra visitor numbers over time.
