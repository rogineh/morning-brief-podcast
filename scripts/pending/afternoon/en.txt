Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Friday, the 23rd of October, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, British banking giant Barclays has announced it will cut around five thousand jobs, roughly five per cent of its global workforce, as artificial intelligence and other technology allow the bank to reduce costs. The bank's chief financial officer said about a thousand roles have already gone, with the rest of the cuts expected to be completed by the end of the year, and roughly sixty per cent of the losses falling within support functions rather than customer-facing roles. The announcement came alongside third-quarter results showing profit up twelve per cent on the same period last year, though underlying trading performance fell short of analyst expectations, with the bank's chief executive describing himself as disappointed with fixed income trading in particular. Barclays described the job losses in its own results materials as organisational simplification and technology change efficiency driven by artificial intelligence, language analysts say is becoming increasingly common across the banking sector as firms look to automate back-office and technical functions. The announcement adds to a growing list of major employers citing AI-driven efficiency as a direct factor in workforce reductions, fuelling ongoing debate about how much of the current wave of job cuts reflects genuine productivity gains from the technology versus cost-cutting that would likely have happened regardless, with AI simply providing convenient cover.

Turning to national news, a major government review has found the Snowy 2.0 hydropower project could ultimately cost up to twenty five billion dollars, roughly twelve times its original two billion dollar budget, with completion now pushed back to late 2029. The Commonwealth-owned company behind the project has set a target cost of twenty two billion dollars, while allowing for a further three billion dollars in contingency funding if needed, and its chief executive has attributed much of the blowout to design immaturity and the sheer complexity of the underground project, while arguing that moving away from a fixed-price contract has actually limited how much worse the overrun could have been. The federal opposition has described the latest cost revision as alarming and wants further detail from the company when it appears before a Senate estimates hearing next week, while the government says it's still working through the full implications of the review and will provide a more complete response in due course. The project was first announced back in 2017 with a budget of just two billion dollars and an original completion target of 2021, meaning the current estimate represents a cost increase, and multi-year delay, that has unfolded gradually over nearly a decade of construction.

In Queensland, Queensland Health says it's now engaging with the nurses' union after staffing shortages forced Townsville University Hospital's cardiothoracic unit, which handles heart and lung surgery, to go on bypass for ten days last month, with five urgent patients flown to Brisbane for treatment during that period. The union representing affected nurses says staff were being inappropriately called in on their days off, leading to fatigue-related sick leave that compounded the original staffing problem, while a hospital spokesperson says there are robust processes in place to manage fatigue and that it's working through the concerns via a standard engagement process. The Australian Medical Association's Queensland branch has pushed back on some specific claims about why nurses were being called in, while separately renewing its own long-standing call for the state government to produce a clear, long-term health workforce plan. Townsville University Hospital is the main referral centre for heart and lung surgery across the whole of north Queensland, meaning any disruption to its cardiothoracic unit has flow-on effects for patients across a wide geographic area who would otherwise need to travel much further for specialist care.

And on the Gold Coast, Mayor Tom Tate has accidentally revealed confidential council plans to buy a strategically significant property inside a state government precinct at Southport, after councillors had been discussing the proposed purchase behind closed doors. At Thursday's public council meeting, councillors debated how to fund the acquisition, weighing up options including drawing on the council's ninety six million dollar investment fund, taking on additional borrowing, or selling off other council-owned property to help cover the cost. The council ultimately voted to support proceeding with the purchase, subject to a further detailed report due back before councillors in November before any final commitment is locked in. The slip has drawn attention to how land acquisitions of this kind are typically negotiated well away from public view until a deal is close to being finalised, a practice councils generally defend as necessary to avoid driving up the asking price once a seller knows council is interested. Local residents will be watching closely for November's report to understand exactly what the council is planning for the site and how ratepayers will ultimately be asked to fund it.
