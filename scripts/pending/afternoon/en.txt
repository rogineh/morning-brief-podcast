In technology, Google's parent company Alphabet has confirmed that its free cash flow turned negative for the first time since its stock market listing more than two decades ago, as spending on artificial intelligence infrastructure continues to surge well beyond earlier expectations. The company's capital expenditure has roughly doubled compared with a year earlier, driven largely by massive investment in data centres and computing hardware needed to train and run increasingly capable AI systems, with executives now expecting total spending for the year to reach around two hundred and five billion dollars, up from earlier guidance closer to one hundred and eighty billion. The company has leaned on a combination of debt and new share issuance to help fund the gap, raising tens of billions of dollars in fresh capital over recent months rather than relying solely on cash generated from its existing businesses. Executives say they expect the elevated spending to continue, with capital expenditure set to rise again next year as the company races to keep pace with rivals also pouring enormous sums into AI infrastructure. Industry analysts say Alphabet's situation reflects a broader pattern across the technology sector's biggest players, with the four major cloud computing providers together on track to spend well over seven hundred billion dollars this year alone, raising ongoing questions among investors about when, or whether, this unprecedented wave of AI infrastructure spending will translate into returns large enough to justify it.

Turning to national news, the federal government's planned modernisation of Australia's telecommunications laws is continuing to progress, with reforms to the decades-old Telecommunications Act and the legislation governing the National Broadband Network forming part of the broader push to extend multi-gigabit internet access to millions more homes and businesses by the end of the decade. Officials say the final stage of the NBN's fibre upgrade program, once complete, will allow more than eleven million homes and businesses across the country to access multi-gigabit-capable broadband, a substantial jump in available speeds compared with the service many households have relied on for years. The legislative modernisation is intended to bring the regulatory framework into line with how telecommunications infrastructure and competition actually operate today, more than a decade after the original laws were first drafted. Industry figures say updating the legal framework alongside the physical network upgrades is just as important as the infrastructure work itself, arguing that outdated rules can otherwise slow innovation and create uncertainty for companies looking to invest in new services. Consumer groups have broadly welcomed the push for faster broadband access, while noting that affordability and reliable service in regional and remote areas remain just as important as raw headline speeds for many Australian households and businesses.

In Queensland, the state's volunteer marine rescue organisation has continued settling into its new dedicated training academy on the Gold Coast, a facility opened earlier this year to serve more than a thousand volunteers as part of the broader consolidation of the state's various marine rescue groups into one unified statewide organisation. The academy gives trainees hands-on experience in a range of realistic conditions, including notoriously tricky bar crossings and complex buoyage systems, preparing volunteers for the kind of real-world rescue scenarios they're likely to encounter on the water. The facility has already hosted training sessions drawing volunteers from right across the state, including some who travelled from as far away as Far North Queensland to take part in advanced instruction sessions. Marine safety officials say having a single, purpose-built training hub makes it far easier to maintain consistent standards across the organisation's volunteer base, compared with the patchwork of informal arrangements that existed when the state's rescue groups operated more independently. The broader consolidation effort continues, with more local rescue units progressively transitioning into the unified structure, a process authorities say ultimately strengthens the safety net available to boaters and other water users across the state.

And on the Gold Coast, the city is gearing up for the return of the Mayor's Christmas Carols at HOTA, with the free, family-friendly event set to mark its fifteenth year running later this week with two nights of festive entertainment at the venue's outdoor stage and parklands. Organisers say the event has become one of the clearest markers of the start of the festive season for many Gold Coast families, drawing large crowds each year for an evening of live music, community choirs, and the usual appearances from some of Christmas's more recognisable characters. Local businesses around the HOTA precinct say events like the carols provide a welcome boost to foot traffic and trade in the lead-up to the busiest retail period of the year, with many families making a night of it by combining the carols with dinner or drinks nearby. Council officials say the event's longevity reflects just how embedded it's become in the city's cultural calendar, with many attendees now bringing along a second or even third generation of family members who grew up going to the carols themselves. With Christmas now just a month away, organisers say the carols mark the unofficial start of a busy run of festive events across the city through to the new year.
