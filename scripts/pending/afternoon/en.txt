Apple is reportedly using Google's Gemini model to power a significantly upgraded version of Siri, after years of criticism that its voice assistant had fallen well behind rival products from other major tech companies. The arrangement would see Apple effectively outsource some of the underlying intelligence behind Siri to Google, rather than relying solely on its own in-house models, in what analysts describe as a pragmatic trade-off between speed to market and long-term control over the technology. Meanwhile, Meta is reportedly planning to cut around ten per cent of the staff in its Reality Labs division, the unit responsible for virtual reality headsets and its Horizon Worlds platform, even as the company continues pouring enormous sums into artificial intelligence more broadly, having spent an estimated seventy five billion dollars on AI last year alone. Industry watchers say the contrast between the two moves highlights just how unevenly big tech's AI-era spending is being distributed, with some bets paying off handsomely while others are quietly being wound back. For Meta specifically, the Reality Labs cuts suggest the company is increasingly prepared to scale back its longer-term metaverse ambitions in order to free up cash and staff for the more immediately commercial AI race, a notable shift from just a couple of years ago when virtual reality was positioned as the centrepiece of its future.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The federal government's additional one point seven billion dollar boost for public hospitals continues to flow through to states and territories this year, with the funding specifically targeted at reducing elective surgery waiting lists and emergency department ramping, where ambulances are forced to queue outside hospitals because there's no immediate capacity to bring patients inside. Health officials say the extra money is helping at the margins, but peak medical bodies continue to warn that structural pressures on the system, including an ageing population and workforce shortages, mean funding boosts alone won't be enough to fix ramping and waiting list problems on a lasting basis. The federal and state governments remain in ongoing negotiations over the next five year hospital funding agreement, which will determine how costs are split between Canberra and the states well beyond this current round of top-up funding. State health ministers have used recent meetings to press the Commonwealth for a bigger overall share of hospital funding, arguing that the states are increasingly being left to absorb cost growth that's outpacing the current funding formula, while the federal government maintains any changes to the split need to be weighed carefully against broader budget pressures.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

Construction planning continues to firm up for the state's 2032 Olympic venues, with the government's preferred plan centred on a new sixty thousand seat stadium and national aquatics centre at Victoria Park in inner Brisbane, alongside a smaller boutique stadium at the Brisbane Showgrounds. The plan, the third iteration put forward since Brisbane won hosting rights, controversially reverses an earlier commitment not to build any new stadiums for the games and will see the Gabba demolished after hosting cricket during the Olympics rather than being redeveloped as originally proposed. Supporters say the revised approach delivers better long-term infrastructure and value for money than retrofitting an ageing venue, while critics, including some Indigenous community representatives, have raised concerns about the Victoria Park site's cultural significance and the process used to select it. The government says further detail on construction timelines and costs will be released as individual venue designs are finalised over the coming months, and that locking in a stable venue plan now is essential to avoid the kind of late-stage cost blowouts that have plagued previous Olympic host cities around the world.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The state government has unlocked land for close to nineteen thousand new homes across the Gold Coast's growth corridors, backing the move with a hundred and forty six million dollar injection into essential infrastructure including sewerage, roads, and power in areas such as Robina, Pimpama, and Coomera. The funding also includes an upgrade to the Merrimac Sewage Treatment Plant, which officials say is necessary to support the sheer scale of new housing planned across the city's northern and central growth areas over the coming years. Property developers have welcomed the announcement as removing a key handbrake on new housing supply, while some planning experts note that unlocking land is only part of the equation, and that ensuring enough tradespeople and construction materials are available to actually build the homes will be just as important to watch in the years ahead. Local councillors say they'll be monitoring how quickly developers actually move once the infrastructure is in place, noting that unlocked land sitting undeveloped for years has been a recurring frustration in past growth-area rollouts elsewhere in the state.
