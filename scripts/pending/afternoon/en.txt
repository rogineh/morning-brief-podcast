Good afternoon, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Friday, the 27th of November, 2026. Here's what's happening today in technology and AI, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, AI company Anthropic has begun rolling out a beta feature that lets its Claude assistant join video calls as an active participant rather than simply answering questions on the side. The new tool, launched initially on Google Meet, lets a user invite Claude into a meeting or have it join automatically, where it appears as a visible participant with its own status indicator alongside the human attendees. During the call, the assistant can generate working documents and whiteboards in real time based on the discussion, and afterwards produces a summary and transcript, though the company says it does not retain the underlying meeting audio itself. The feature is currently limited to business and enterprise subscribers, with free and individual paid users excluded for now, and organisations using certain high-security configurations are also left out of the beta while the company works through the implications. Companion tools allowing the assistant to build shared documents and whiteboards outside of meetings have launched alongside it, reflecting a broader push by major AI companies to move their assistants from simple chat interfaces into more active roles across everyday workplace software. Industry analysts say embedding AI directly into live meetings represents one of the more significant tests yet of whether workers are comfortable having an AI system actively present during real-time conversations, rather than just processing information after the fact.

Turning to national news, the Federal Court has found that Coles misled customers over its "Down Down" discount campaign, ruling the supermarket giant made false or misleading representations about price reductions across more than two hundred commonly purchased products. The case, brought by the competition watchdog, centred on claims that items marketed as being permanently discounted had in some instances actually been sold at higher prices immediately beforehand, creating a false impression of ongoing savings for shoppers. The court will determine penalties and any other orders at a later date, with Coles saying it is reviewing the judgment and considering its options. A similar case against Woolworths over its own "Prices Dropped" campaign remains before the court, with that matter centred on a smaller bundle of everyday products drawn from a longer list the watchdog originally raised concerns about. The competition regulator has said it will not comment further on the Woolworths proceedings while the matter remains active, though both supermarkets are understood to face further procedural deadlines in the coming days as the cases progress. Consumer advocates have welcomed the Coles ruling as a significant finding, arguing it sends a clear signal to major retailers that marketing claims about discounts and price reductions will face genuine scrutiny rather than being taken at face value.

In Queensland, wildlife conservationist Robert Irwin has been named the state's Australian of the Year for 2027, heading a list of winners recognised for their contributions across conservation, sport, disability advocacy, and community service. Irwin, who has spent years continuing his family's conservation work at Australia Zoo, was recognised for raising the profile of wildlife protection both in Australia and internationally. Joining him on the list, sprinter Gout Gout of Ipswich was named the state's Young Australian of the Year following a breakout year on the track, while Sunshine Coast disability advocate Carmel Crouch was named Senior Australian of the Year for her decades of work supporting people with disability. Noel Brown, a First Nations hospital greeter from East Trinity, was recognised as the state's Local Hero for his quiet, consistent work welcoming and supporting patients and families at a difficult time. All four Queensland recipients now progress to the national Australian of the Year awards, which will be announced in Canberra at the end of January. Organisers say this year's list reflects a particularly broad spread of achievement, from high-profile conservation and sporting success through to the kind of everyday community contribution that often goes unrecognised.

And on the Gold Coast, the city's lifeguard service is gearing up for an expanded summer patrol season, with around two hundred and eighty lifeguards set to cover forty-two beaches daily once the main patrol period begins next month. The extended hours and wider coverage follow a summer in which lifeguards carried out well over a thousand rescues across the city's beaches, and come after authorities flagged renewed concern about visitors swimming outside patrolled areas and underestimating how quickly calm-looking conditions can turn dangerous. This year's safety campaign is placing particular emphasis on education for groups identified as being at higher risk of getting into trouble in the water, including visitors less familiar with surf conditions along the open coastline. Lifeguard services say the message remains simple but constant, with swimmers urged to stick between the flags and check conditions before entering the water, even on days that look deceptively calm from the shore. Local tourism operators say the expanded patrols are a welcome reassurance for both residents and the large number of domestic and international visitors expected along the coast over the holiday period. With the main patrol season just weeks away, authorities say preparations are well underway to have the full lifeguard roster in place in time for the summer school holiday rush.
