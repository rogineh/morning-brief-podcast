In technology, Microsoft has offered voluntary buyout packages to roughly sixty thousand employees worldwide, equivalent to around a quarter of its entire global workforce, as part of a broader cost-cutting push that the company has directly linked to its enormous ongoing spending on artificial intelligence. The offer covers staff at vice president level and below across selected teams in the United States, Canada, India, and the United Kingdom, though certain groups, including some data centre, sales, and marketing roles, have been excluded from eligibility. Employees who want to take the package have until early December to decide, with anyone accepting the offer set to leave the company at the very end of the year under what's being described as a standard severance arrangement. The move follows a smaller voluntary retirement program Microsoft ran earlier this year for a limited group of US staff, as well as a separate round of around forty eight hundred job cuts announced back in July. Importantly, the sixty thousand figure represents how many employees are eligible to apply, not how many are actually expected to accept, meaning the real scale of departures likely won't become clear until well after the December deadline passes.

Turning to national news, all eyes are on new inflation figures released today, with economists broadly expecting underlying quarterly inflation to come in at around one per cent, slightly above the Reserve Bank's own earlier forecast of zero point eight per cent. The Reserve Bank governor also appeared before a Senate committee today, with markets now pricing in a reasonably strong chance of an interest rate increase when the board next meets in November. A stronger than expected inflation read would likely reinforce the case for the central bank to act, while a softer number could ease pressure and buy policymakers a little more time before raising rates again. The data lands at a politically sensitive moment, with the government facing ongoing pressure over cost of living and housing affordability, and any further increase in borrowing costs likely to draw immediate criticism from across the political spectrum. Financial markets reacted cautiously throughout the day, with earlier gains on the local share market pared back as traders digested the numbers and recalibrated their expectations for the Reserve Bank's next move.

In Queensland, Toowoomba Regional Council has voted to grant customer-owned bank People First Bank a one million dollar discount on standard infrastructure charges, as part of a scheme designed to encourage new development in the city's central business district. The discount was applied through an existing council incentive program aimed at attracting commercial investment into the CBD, though at least one councillor has publicly questioned the scale of the concession, reportedly describing it as roughly equivalent in value to a one per cent increase in general council rates for the wider community. Supporters of the scheme argue that encouraging major financial institutions to invest directly in the city centre delivers broader economic benefits over time, including jobs and foot traffic, that can ultimately outweigh the upfront cost of the discount itself. Critics counter that ratepayers deserve more transparency about exactly how these kinds of incentive deals are costed and justified before councillors sign off on them. The council's development incentive scheme has been used previously to attract other commercial projects into the CBD, suggesting today's decision is unlikely to be the last time ratepayers hear about a significant infrastructure charge concession granted under the same program.

And on the Gold Coast, the city council has voted to cap the incoming chief executive's independent spending authority at one hundred million dollars for major decisions such as land purchases, pending full council endorsement, following the abrupt resignation of long-serving chief executive Tim Baker three months ago. The committee responsible for the change said the new cap strikes a reasonable balance between appropriate councillor oversight and the kind of bureaucratic efficiency a council of the Gold Coast's size genuinely needs to function properly day to day. Baker's departure in July followed sustained criticism over his use of ratepayer-funded overseas travel, with one review reportedly identifying more than one hundred thousand dollars in travel expenses linked to him over just a twelve month period, though some councillors have separately defended his record and capability during his five years in the role. An acting chief executive has been running the organisation since Baker's exit, with the council now expecting to appoint a permanent replacement sometime early next year. The new spending cap will apply regardless of who ultimately takes on the top job, reflecting a broader push from some councillors to tighten financial oversight at the city's largest local government organisation following the upheaval of recent months.
