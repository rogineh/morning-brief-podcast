First, technology and AI. Anthropic and Accenture have announced a major new partnership to build a team of embedded evaluators who'll work directly inside Anthropic alongside its own safety teams, red-teaming models, running alignment assessments, and stress-testing safeguards before systems are released. The arrangement will be led by Accenture's specialist AI unit, Faculty, and gives outside evaluators access comparable to an employee's, letting them watch models take shape during training and speak directly with the staff making key decisions about how those systems are built and deployed. Each company is expected to invest at least a billion dollars over the next five years into the effort, which industry watchers are describing as the first concrete step in a broader plan from Anthropic's leadership to slow the pace of frontier AI development through independent oversight rather than self-regulation alone. The move comes at a moment of unusually public soul-searching across the AI industry about how fast is too fast, with rival labs facing growing pressure to explain their own safety testing regimes in similarly transparent terms. Analysts note that having a major consulting firm embedded this deeply inside a frontier AI lab is unprecedented, and will be watched closely as a test case for whether outside scrutiny can genuinely keep pace with the speed of internal model development, rather than simply reviewing systems after key decisions have already been made.

Now to national news here in Australia. Treasurer Jim Chalmers has released a new Intergenerational Report forecasting the Australian economy will more than double in size over coming decades, with artificial intelligence expected to fundamentally reshape how the country works, produces, and grows. The report also projects Australia's population will approach forty million people by 2066, driven mainly by longer lifespans even as the birthrate continues falling faster than earlier forecasts anticipated, a combination Treasury says will reshape everything from the aged care system to the tax base over the coming generation. Separately, financial markets spent the day digesting fresh commentary from the country's biggest banks, with both Commonwealth Bank and ANZ now bringing forward their forecasts for the next interest rate move, as investors weigh the outlook for household budgets against a backdrop of persistent global uncertainty. The revised timing has reignited debate among economists over how much further relief mortgage holders can realistically expect in the near term, with some warning that other cost pressures, including fuel and grocery prices, could offset any benefit from rate movements later in the year.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. The Queensland Police Service has welcomed a fresh intake of newly sworn officers after its largest graduation ceremony of the year at the Brisbane Police Academy, with well over a hundred recruits formally joining the force following months of training. Senior police leadership addressed the graduating class directly, emphasising the scale of community trust that comes with the badge and the growing complexity of frontline policing work across the state, from road safety operations to family violence response, and said the fresh numbers would help ease pressure on stations that have faced staffing strain in recent years. Separately, new figures show only a small fraction of the state's councils currently offer kerbside green waste collection, prompting renewed calls from environmental groups for broader rollout of food and garden organics bins as the state looks to cut the volume of waste heading to landfill. Advocates argue that expanding the scheme statewide could significantly cut emissions from organic waste breaking down in landfill, while several regional councils have pointed to the upfront cost of new bins and collection trucks as the main barrier holding back a faster rollout.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. The SWELL Sculpture Festival has wrapped up its ten-day run along Currumbin Beach, capping off what organisers are calling one of its most successful years yet, with more than a hundred large-scale sculptures and a gallery of smaller works drawing crowds to the free open-air exhibition. This year's program was headlined by internationally renowned artist Patricia Piccinini, whose thought-provoking pieces have previously featured in major galleries around the world, alongside guided walks, artist talks, and hands-on workshops that ran throughout the festival's stay on the coastline. Local tourism operators say the event continues to be one of the standout drawcards on the Gold Coast's spring calendar, bringing visitors from well beyond the region and giving nearby cafes and businesses a welcome boost during what's traditionally a quieter stretch between the winter and summer tourist seasons.
