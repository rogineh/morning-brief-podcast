Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Friday, the 6th of November, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, global markets suffered a brutal sell-off overnight, with the tech-heavy Nasdaq index plunging more than six per cent and wiping out roughly two-point-three trillion dollars in market value in a single session. Investors reacted to a combination of factors, including unexpected results from the United States midterm elections that saw a wave of openly anti-AI candidates win seats, alongside renewed anxiety about whether the enormous sums being poured into artificial intelligence infrastructure will ever generate matching returns. The sell-off hit major technology and chip companies particularly hard, as traders reassessed political risk to the AI sector just as several of the industry's biggest players prepare to report their latest spending figures in the coming days. Market analysts say the scale of the overnight losses reflects just how closely intertwined broader market sentiment has become with confidence in the AI investment story specifically, meaning any crack in that confidence now has the potential to ripple through markets far beyond the technology sector itself. Whether today's losses mark a genuine turning point or simply a sharp but temporary correction remains very much an open question, with markets likely to stay volatile in the days ahead as investors digest both the political signal from the midterms and upcoming corporate earnings.

Turning to national news, a political fight has broken out over where large-scale data centres should actually be built in Australia, after the Deputy Prime Minister said today that suburban areas are simply the wrong location for this kind of major digital infrastructure. Speaking this morning, the Deputy Prime Minister argued that data centres and the substantial energy and water demands that come with them need to be planned well away from established residential communities, pointing to growing community pushback against several proposed projects around the country. The federal Nationals leader seized on the comments to renew his own criticism of the government's broader approach to data centre development, describing the current national strategy as posing a serious risk to the stability of the country's wider energy system. The exchange comes as several enormous data centre projects continue working through planning and approval processes in various states, with questions about appropriate siting, energy demand, and water usage becoming an increasingly prominent flashpoint in Australian politics. Separately, the federal government's Productivity Commission released interim findings today from its broader inquiry into business dynamism, though the inquiry was specifically directed not to examine either tax settings or industrial relations as part of its scope, limiting how far its eventual recommendations might reach.

In Queensland, a proposed thirty-two billion dollar data centre precinct in the state's Western Downs region has secured both a builder and an initial grid power connection, even though the local council still hasn't formally approved the project's development application. The facility, anchored by a major artificial intelligence company as its primary tenant, would connect directly into high-voltage transmission infrastructure near an existing electricity generation precinct rather than drawing from the local distribution network serving nearby homes and businesses. Depending on which measure is used, the completed project could eventually draw peak power equivalent to what one-and-a-half million average Australian households use, a scale that's prompted some analysts to suggest it alone could increase the state's overall daily electricity consumption by as much as twenty-five per cent. The regional council's own mayor has been careful to stress that approval is genuinely not yet locked in, with the development application still sitting before the council's planning team for proper assessment. If ultimately approved, construction itself could take anywhere from four to six years to complete, meaning any direct economic or energy impact on the region remains some years away regardless of how today's political debate over data centre siting eventually plays out.

And on the Gold Coast, the city recorded its hottest November day in thirty years yesterday, with the temperature at the Seaway reaching thirty-five-point-seven degrees in the early afternoon, well above the previous November record set back in 1995. The unusually intense heat came just hours before an overnight power outage caused by a substation fault left around twelve thousand properties across the city without electricity from half past midnight, stretching from Broadbeach through to Miami. Energex crews worked through the night to restore supply, with most affected customers back online within an hour, though a smaller group of around a hundred and twenty properties at Burleigh Heads remained without power until six o'clock this morning. The network operator hasn't linked the substation fault directly to yesterday's extreme heat, though the timing of an unusually hot day followed almost immediately by a significant electrical fault has nonetheless drawn some local attention. With forecasters already warning that this summer could bring further unusually intense heat events to the region, residents are being reminded to stay alert for both heat-health warnings and potential strain on local electricity infrastructure as the warmer months properly get underway.
