Investment in AI chip startups looking to challenge Nvidia's dominance has continued flowing steadily this year, with two separate companies recently closing substantial funding rounds built around alternative approaches to AI processing hardware. One Nevada-based challenger secured two hundred and thirty million dollars from backers including Arm Holdings and the Qatar Investment Authority, while a separate chip company led by a former investment banker raised two hundred and twenty five million dollars from investors including SoftBank at a valuation of around four hundred million dollars. Industry analysts say both rounds reflect continued investor appetite for genuine alternatives to Nvidia's chips, even though Nvidia itself remains by far the dominant supplier of the processors underpinning most large AI systems today. Chip industry veterans caution that unseating an incumbent with as much of a head start as Nvidia has built up will take years rather than months, but say the sheer scale of capital still willing to back credible challengers suggests investors see real room in the market for more than one winner.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The federal government has confirmed Australia will become the first country outside the United States to operate the AIM-260 Joint Advanced Tactical Missile, backing the roughly seven hundred and thirty six million dollar acquisition through the US foreign military sales program. The missile will first be fitted to the Royal Australian Air Force's Super Hornet fleet, before later being integrated with the F-35A Lightning and the Growler electronic warfare aircraft. Defence officials say the missile represents a meaningful upgrade in long range air-to-air capability, and that securing early access ahead of many other allied nations reflects the closeness of Australia's defence relationship with the United States. Defence analysts say the acquisition fits a broader pattern of the government prioritising high-end strike and missile capability as a core plank of its defence strategy, alongside the large-scale conventional investments already committed under this year's defence funding plan.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

Former Supreme Court Chief Justice Thomas Bradley has been formally appointed as the twenty eighth Governor of Queensland, taking on the state's senior vice-regal role after a long career on the bench. The appointment follows the standard process of the Premier recommending a candidate to the King, and the role itself is largely ceremonial, though the governor retains certain constitutional responsibilities including swearing in the government after an election and granting royal assent to legislation. Legal figures have broadly welcomed the appointment, noting Bradley's extensive judicial experience makes him well placed to navigate the role's occasional constitutional sensitivities. The governor's office says an official swearing-in ceremony will be held in the coming weeks, with further detail on the new governor's specific priorities for the role expected to follow once he formally takes up the position.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The City of Gold Coast has handed down its two point seven billion dollar budget for the current financial year, with the headline general rate rise set at four point seven per cent after councillors wound back an initially proposed five point seven per cent increase following internal pushback. The revised, lower rate rise is expected to save ratepayers a combined total of at least thirteen million dollars compared with the original proposal, with councillors also opting to retain the existing ten per cent early payment discount rather than replacing it with a flat rate alternative that had been floated earlier in the budget process. The Mayor says the budget was shaped against a backdrop of difficult global economic conditions and persistent inflation, which has pushed up the council's own costs for labour, fuel, and contracted services just as it has for households across the city. Ratepayer groups have welcomed the wound-back increase as a sign councillors are listening to community pressure on cost of living, while some have questioned whether the council will need to revisit rates again sooner than planned if cost pressures on its own operations continue to build.
