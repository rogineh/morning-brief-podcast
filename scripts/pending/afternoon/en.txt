Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Monday, the 2nd of November, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, OpenAI has confirmed that from December, its ChatGPT and Codex products will draw from a single, shared usage allowance measured in tokens, rather than the separate limits that currently apply to each product individually. The change will affect subscribers on the company's Plus, Pro, and Business plans, meaning anyone using both the ChatGPT assistant and the Codex coding tool heavily in the same billing period could see their combined usage capped sooner than it would be under the current separate-allowance system. OpenAI's help centre update stopped short of specifying an exact token amount for each subscription tier, or confirming precisely which day in December the change takes effect, leaving some uncertainty for business customers trying to plan around the new system. The company has indicated that simple chat interactions won't draw from the shared pool at all, and that users will be able to track their combined remaining balance and reset times through a dedicated usage section in their account settings once the change goes live. The move comes as OpenAI and its competitors continue adjusting pricing and usage models across their product lines, reflecting the broader tension in the industry between offering generous access to build loyal users and managing the genuinely enormous computing costs behind running these systems at scale.

Turning to national news, Australian share markets dipped today as investors braced for an expected interest rate rise from the Reserve Bank tomorrow, with the benchmark index down around zero-point-six per cent, dragged lower by weakness across the banking and mining sectors. Westpac added to the day's news flow by reporting a six-point-eight billion dollar annual profit, though attention across the market remained firmly fixed on tomorrow's Reserve Bank decision, which most economists and traders now expect will deliver a quarter-of-a-percentage-point increase to the cash rate. Several major banks have already shifted their own forecasts in recent days to reflect the higher likelihood of a rate rise, with some institutions revising down their broader economic growth expectations at the same time, citing the cumulative drag that repeated rate increases this year are having on household spending and business investment. If tomorrow's increase goes ahead as expected, it would mark the central bank's fifth rate rise so far this year, extending a run of monetary tightening that's already weighed heavily on mortgage holders and contributed to the sharpest fall in national home values in roughly four decades. Markets will be watching closely not just for the decision itself but for any accompanying commentary from the Reserve Bank governor about the likely path of interest rates heading into next year.

In Queensland, Justice Thomas Bradley has been sworn in as the state's twenty-eighth governor in a ceremony held at state parliament, becoming the first openly gay man to hold the largely ceremonial but historically significant position. In his first address after taking on the role, the new governor said he intends to spend as much time outside Brisbane as he does within it, signalling a desire to connect directly with regional and remote communities across the state during his term. Premier David Crisafulli described the appointment as a significant moment for Queensland, praising the new governor's legal background and his stated commitment to representing the whole state rather than focusing primarily on the capital. Justice Bradley succeeds Doctor Jeannette Young, who served as governor through a period that included some of the state's most challenging recent years, and takes on a role that includes opening parliament, granting royal assent to legislation, and representing the state at major ceremonial occasions. While the governor's office carries limited direct political power under Australia's system of government, the position still holds considerable symbolic weight, and today's swearing-in ceremony drew senior figures from across Queensland's political, legal, and community sectors.

And on the Gold Coast, city councillors are considering a proposal to trial artificial lighting along sections of beach after dark specifically to extend surfing hours, with the plan recommended for use during next year's World Surf League Championship Tour event at Snapper Rocks. A report prepared for the council's Transport and Infrastructure Committee outlined how the lighting trial could work, framing it as a way to make the most of world-class surf conditions outside daylight hours during one of the city's marquee sporting events. However, the proposal has already drawn concern from at least one marine researcher, who warns that artificial lighting could meaningfully increase the risk of shark encounters in the water after dark. The Bond University researcher explained that the lights would likely attract smaller fish and squid, which in turn could draw in tiger sharks, a species known for hunting primarily at night, potentially increasing the chances of an encounter between sharks and night-time surfers. The idea of night surfing on the Gold Coast isn't entirely new, having been floated informally by local surfing groups more than a decade ago, though any formal council-endorsed trial would represent a significant step beyond those earlier, more casual conversations about extending the city's surfing hours after sunset.
