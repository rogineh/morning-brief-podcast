Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Thursday, the 29th of October, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, Google has unveiled Gemini 4 Carbon, a new frontier AI model specifically designed with a strong focus on cybersecurity, including finding software vulnerabilities, developing exploits, and helping patch flaws before attackers can take advantage of them. The company says the model has set new benchmark results across several cybersecurity-specific tests, including one focused purely on vulnerability discovery, though independent experts note these figures come from controlled testing environments rather than real-world deployment. Rather than releasing the model broadly straight away, Google is rolling it out first to a select group of cyber defenders, before gradually extending access to trusted partners through an existing program that already includes government agencies, national cyber security bodies, and operators of critical infrastructure. Ordinary developers, businesses, and everyday users won't get access for some time yet, with Google giving no firm public release date, though the company's previous comparable model moved from a similar restricted defender-only launch to full public availability within roughly two weeks. The approach reflects a broader industry pattern of treating powerful new AI capabilities as something to trial cautiously with trusted security specialists first, given the same tools capable of finding and patching vulnerabilities could, at least in theory, also be misused to find and exploit them.

Turning to national news, Queensland's Supreme Court has dismissed a conservation group's legal challenge against the long-running Alpha Coal Project in the Galilee Basin, a venture associated with mining magnate Gina Rinehart and her Indian joint venture partner. The specific grounds and reasoning behind today's ruling haven't yet been detailed publicly, though the case follows a long history of legal challenges against the project stretching back more than a decade, including earlier unsuccessful attempts to block it on climate and environmental grounds. In a related development, a separate coal project in the same region, roughly fifty kilometres north of Alpha, has gone into liquidation after its parent company appointed liquidators earlier this week, highlighting the mixed fortunes facing different Galilee Basin coal ventures even as some projects continue clearing legal hurdles. Environmental groups have long argued that large-scale coal projects in the Galilee Basin carry outsized climate consequences given the sheer volume of coal involved, while proponents maintain the projects deliver substantial jobs and royalties for the state. Today's court decision is likely to be seen as a win for the project's backers, even as the broader economic and political debate over the future of coal mining in the region continues largely unresolved.

In Queensland, child safety and youth justice workers walked off the job across the south-east of the state at midday today, in what the Together Union describes as the longest stop-work action in the sector since 2018, after enterprise bargaining negotiations with the state government collapsed. Striking workers marched to Parliament House following the walkout, with the union saying the government has failed to put a genuine new offer on the table despite months of negotiations that began back in March. The union says many child safety officers are routinely carrying caseloads of thirty or more children, nearly double what it considers a safe workload of around sixteen, with more than a hundred child protection positions and dozens of youth justice roles currently sitting vacant across the state. A government spokesperson said contingency plans were in place to minimise disruption from today's action, while continuing to characterise ongoing negotiations as being conducted in good faith. The dispute adds to a difficult few months for the child safety portfolio more broadly, following earlier criticism this year over troubled IT systems and funding cuts that contributed to a separate ministerial resignation.

And on the Gold Coast, the United Workers Union has renewed warnings that thousands of casino jobs remain at risk following this week's two hundred and sixty million dollar Federal Court fine against Star Entertainment, with the union representing roughly nine thousand workers across the company's Brisbane, Gold Coast, and Sydney properties. The union's casino director said ordinary workers should not be made to pay the price for what the court found were serious corporate compliance failures, calling on the financial crimes regulator to work with Star on a realistic payment arrangement rather than risk pushing the company toward collapse. Star had already warned before today's ruling that a fine much above one hundred million dollars could threaten its ongoing viability, and the company required an emergency rescue package from an international gambling operator just last year to avoid administration altogether. For Gold Coast workers specifically, the uncertainty adds to what's already been a difficult stretch for the local casino, which remains one of the city's larger private employers and a significant draw for tourism-related spending in the Surfers Paradise precinct. Unions and the company are expected to continue talks in coming weeks as Star works through exactly how it intends to fund the penalty without resorting to the kind of workforce cuts its staff are now bracing for.
