First, technology and AI. New reporting today details how Australia's Defence Force is rapidly weaving artificial intelligence into everything from drone operations to intelligence analysis, with officials describing the shift as building something close to a "silicon commander" capable of processing vast amounts of information far faster than human analysts ever could. The military's autonomous Ghost Bat aircraft, which requires no human pilot at the controls or joystick operator on the ground, is held up as an early example of the approach, though defence officials stress it currently runs on deterministic programming rather than adaptive AI, reflecting deliberate caution about handing over too much decision-making authority to systems that aren't fully explainable. That caution stems partly from overseas experience, with a recent Pentagon investigation into a deadly missile strike finding that operators had leaned too heavily on an AI targeting system's recommendations without properly verifying outdated intelligence, a phenomenon researchers call automation bias. Australia's own military AI policy now requires systems to be explainable, reliable, and secure, with built-in safeguards against unintended bias, as the country tries to capture the efficiency benefits of AI in defence without repeating mistakes made elsewhere. Analysts say the challenge for Australia and its allies over the coming years won't just be building capable military AI systems, but ensuring the humans working alongside them retain both the time and the institutional incentive to actually scrutinise machine recommendations rather than simply deferring to them under operational pressure.

Now to national news here in Australia. A hazard reduction burn near Wedderburn in Sydney's south-west broke containment this afternoon after winds strengthened suddenly, destroying one Rural Fire Service truck and seriously damaging two others as flames overran the vehicles on Katanna Road. One firefighter suffering smoke effects was taken to hospital, and a further eighteen personnel were assessed at the scene, with crews describing themselves as shaken by how quickly conditions turned against them during what had been a routine planned burn. The safety regulator has been notified and an investigation into exactly how the burn escaped control is now under way, with the incident likely to prompt renewed scrutiny of hazard reduction protocols heading into what authorities have already warned could be a demanding bushfire season across the country's east. Fire authorities say incidents like today's underline the inherent risk crews take on even during routine, carefully planned burns, and that conditions can shift from manageable to dangerous within minutes once wind patterns change unexpectedly.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. The Brisbane Broncos have booked their spot in next weekend's NRLW grand final after defeating the Gold Coast Titans twenty to eight in an entertaining preliminary final at Cbus Super Stadium, setting up a rematch of last year's decider against the Sydney Roosters, who won that earlier contest by four points. Brisbane dominated the opening half, shutting down several of the Titans' key attacking weapons and forcing costly errors, before the home side rallied briefly with a second-half try that ultimately proved too little too late. The result ends what had still been a record-breaking season for the Gold Coast club, and sets up a blockbuster decider next weekend between two of the competition's most successful recent sides. Titans fans and players were left to reflect on a genuinely breakthrough campaign despite the disappointment, with the club having reached its first-ever preliminary final off the back of a near-perfect regular season, giving supporters plenty of reason for optimism heading into next year even without the ultimate prize this time around.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. A major push is under way to reshape how the city sees itself and how the rest of the world sees it, with the newly formed Brand Gold Coast organisation, led by internationally recognised place-branding expert Todd Babiak, working to move the city's identity beyond the long-standing "Glitter Strip" tag. The initiative is designed to be collectively owned by residents, government, and local businesses rather than imposed from the top down, with organisers hoping a sharper sense of identity will help attract fresh investment, talent, and visitors as the city continues its rapid growth ahead of the 2032 Olympic and Paralympic Games.
