Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Sunday, the 25th of October, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, Google has expanded real-time speech translation in Google Meet to cover sixty three languages, with the system now automatically detecting which language a speaker is using rather than requiring everyone on a call to pre-select a single language pair in advance. The update, powered by Google's Gemini AI models, allows different participants in the same meeting to speak different languages and still have what they say translated for everyone else, with translated captions appearing alongside the spoken audio in near real time. The feature remains limited to paid subscribers on Google's AI Pro and Ultra plans or selected Workspace tiers, with usage capped at ninety minutes per meeting on standard plans and up to eight hours for higher-tier subscribers, and it won't yet work for livestreams, recorded meetings, or calls using end-to-end client-side encryption. The rollout began gradually on faster-moving parts of Google's platform earlier this week, with slower, more conservative Workspace domains set to receive the update over the first half of November. Google had previously said its underlying translation model could handle more than seventy languages, so some users have noted the gap between that figure and today's sixty three-language rollout, though the company hasn't explained the difference publicly.

Turning to national news, Royal Darwin Hospital has issued a formal apology after confidential medical information belonging to roughly a dozen pregnant patients was posted to Instagram by a person attached to the hospital's obstetrics and gynaecology unit. Northern Territory Health says the material, shared to an account with around twenty eight thousand followers, included a handover sheet showing patient names, hospital record numbers, ward and bed details, medical histories, and treatment plans, along with footage from an internal staff meeting in which a patient's care was discussed. The individual responsible has been described by the hospital as a medical observer who held no authority to manage patients directly, and whose clinical attachment to the health service has since been terminated following the breach. At least one affected patient has spoken publicly about the exposure, describing it as deeply distressing to learn such personal medical details had been shared so widely without consent. The territory's health department says it's continuing to investigate exactly how the breach occurred and is working through what further steps, including notifying all affected patients individually, still need to be taken.

In Queensland, the federal government has thrown its support behind a trial heat battery project at Rio Tinto's Yarwun alumina refinery near Gladstone, aimed at cutting the plant's reliance on fossil fuels for high-temperature industrial processes. The Australian Renewable Energy Agency is providing an initial three point six million dollars to fund detailed engineering and design work, with up to thirty eight million dollars more potentially available if the project moves ahead to full deployment, and a further contingency allocation available under certain conditions. The technology, developed in partnership with American firm Rondo Energy, would use relatively cheap electricity to heat refractory bricks to extremely high temperatures, storing that heat and later releasing it as steam for the refining process. Rio Tinto estimates the battery could eventually supply around five per cent of the refinery's total steam needs and cut its direct emissions by roughly sixty seven thousand tonnes of carbon dioxide each year, though some energy analysts have noted that figure represents a relatively modest slice of the plant's overall emissions given most of its steam will still come from coal-fired boilers. The federal Climate Change and Energy Minister described the project as an important practical test of whether heat battery technology can be scaled up for heavy industry more broadly, with a final decision on full deployment still some way off pending the engineering study's results.

And on the Gold Coast, Broc Feeney claimed victory in Sunday's second and deciding Gold Coast 500 race, backing up his pole position with a win that capped off a dramatic weekend at Surfers Paradise, though the result is already being overshadowed by controversy over who makes it through to the Supercars championship's upcoming semi-finals. Brodie Kostecki finished second, around seven seconds behind Feeney, with Saturday's race winner Chaz Mostert rounding out the podium in third place. The bigger talking point from the weekend was a tight battle for the final finals qualification spots, with Anton De Pasquale narrowly squeezing through at the expense of Ryan Wood, while Kai Allen and Jack Le Brocq were also eliminated from finals contention, prompting pointed reaction from several of the drivers and teams who missed out. Treacherous wet conditions also forced an early end to one of Sunday's two Porsche Carrera Cup support races, though that didn't stop Harri Jones from wrapping up his third Carrera Cup championship with a round still to spare. With the Gold Coast 500 now finished for another year, attention turns to how the surviving Supercars contenders will fare as the finals series moves to its next round.
