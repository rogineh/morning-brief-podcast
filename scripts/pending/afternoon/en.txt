In technology, the United States has moved to close what officials describe as a major loophole in its artificial intelligence chip export controls, issuing new rules aimed at stopping Chinese firms from accessing advanced American AI computing power through overseas data centres. Under the previous regime, chips themselves were restricted from export to China, but nothing stopped Chinese companies from simply renting remote access to that same computing power sitting in data centres in third countries. The new interim rule closes that gap by imposing licensing requirements on advanced chip exports to a handful of countries that have become popular hosting locations for this kind of remote-access arrangement, with operators already running covered services given a ninety-day window to obtain the necessary licences. The rule is set to take effect once it's formally published, and regulators will accept public submissions on it for sixty days after that. At least one affected government has said it's now consulting with local companies and engaging with American authorities over exactly how the new restrictions will be applied in practice, suggesting there's still real uncertainty about the rule's precise scope. The move effectively scraps a more complex tiered framework for AI chip access that an earlier administration had put in place, replacing it with a narrower rule focused specifically on this remote-access workaround, and underscores just how determined policymakers remain to prevent the most advanced computing power from reaching Chinese developers by any route, direct or indirect.

Turning to national news, fresh figures from the Australian Bureau of Statistics show wages grew one-point-one per cent in the September quarter, the strongest quarterly result in several years, with annual wage growth lifting to three-point-five per cent. Public sector wages grew faster than private sector pay over the year, according to the data, continuing a gradual narrowing of the gap between the two sectors that's been underway for some time. The figures matter because they feed directly into the Reserve Bank's thinking on interest rates, coming only weeks after the central bank lifted the cash rate for the fourth time this year, taking it to four-point-eight-five per cent, as it continues trying to keep inflation in check. With the Reserve Bank's final rate decision for the year due in early December, economists are now weighing whether today's stronger-than-expected wage figures make a further hike more likely, or whether other signs of a cooling labour market will be enough to keep rates on hold. Markets have generally been pricing in only modest rate relief over the next couple of years, and today's data does little to change that picture, with most analysts still expecting any meaningful cuts to be a 2027 story rather than something that arrives before Christmas.

In Queensland, Wildlife Warrior Robert Irwin has been named the state's 2027 Australian of the Year, with his mother Terri accepting the honour on his behalf at a ceremony in Brisbane earlier this week. The award recognises his work carrying on his late father Steve's conservation legacy through Australia Zoo and his broader advocacy for wildlife protection, which has reached a global audience in recent years. He was named alongside three other Queensland recipients: a Sunshine Coast advocate was recognised as Senior Australian of the Year, a young Ipswich sprinter who's rapidly emerged as one of the country's brightest athletic talents was named Young Australian of the Year, and a Far North Queensland community figure was recognised as Local Hero for his volunteer contributions. All four will now go forward as Queensland's representatives at the national Australian of the Year ceremony in Canberra in late January. Premier's department officials say the strength and diversity of this year's field reflects just how many Queenslanders are quietly doing extraordinary work in their communities, often without seeking any recognition at all, and that the annual awards process remains one of the best opportunities to shine a light on exactly that kind of contribution.

And on the Gold Coast, the city has been playing host to an inspiring moment in Australian surfing after fourteen-year-old Leihani Zoric arrived home following her gold medal win at the ISA World Surfing Games in Peru, becoming the youngest world champion in the event's history and the first Indigenous Australian to win the title. Zoric fronted the media on the Gold Coast alongside eight-time world champion Stephanie Gilmore, who says the teenager's rise has helped reignite her own competitive drive as she chases an Olympic surfing debut. Zoric has already set her sights on the Los Angeles Olympics in 2028, saying she hopes her win inspires Indigenous girls and young Australians everywhere to believe they can reach the top of their sport. Local surfing identities say the pairing of Gilmore, long one of the Gold Coast's most recognisable sporting exports, with a rising teenage star captures exactly the kind of generational handover that keeps the city's surfing culture thriving, with junior participation numbers across Gold Coast surf clubs continuing to climb as more young athletes chase similar dreams.
