New analysis suggests China's operational AI computing capacity has climbed to roughly twenty-four gigawatts, closing in on the United States' total of around fifty-six gigawatts, with a further fifty gigawatts reportedly planned or already under construction there. The figures underline just how quickly the global race to build AI computing infrastructure is escalating, with both countries pouring enormous resources into data centres, power generation, and chip supply chains in an effort to stay ahead in what's increasingly being framed as a strategic technology contest rather than simply a commercial one.

Closer to home, Australia's own science agency, the CSIRO, has signed a new agreement with vaccine maker Moderna, aimed at strengthening the country's ability to respond to future biosecurity threats. The partnership will draw on advanced computational tools to help speed up vaccine design and testing, part of a broader push to make sure Australia isn't caught under-prepared the way many countries were during the early stages of the last pandemic.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The Northern Territory's environmental regulator says it has only a "low level of confidence" in the environmental plans submitted for a proposed hyperscale data centre on a remote cattle station in the Barkly region. The project, which would need to draw up to four billion litres of groundwater a year and would be powered partly by gas generators with a capacity several times greater than Darwin's entire peak energy demand, has been sent back to its developer for considerably more detail before any approval can proceed. Environmental groups say the case highlights the growing tension between the enormous water and power demands of new AI infrastructure and the environmental limits of the remote regions increasingly being eyed for these projects.

Meanwhile, cricket fans are gearing up for the first Test between Australia and South Africa, which gets underway this evening at Kingsmead in Durban. It's the opening match of a three-Test series between two of the strongest sides in world cricket, with South African captain Temba Bavuma leading the hosts against Pat Cummins' touring Australians in what's shaping up as an early form guide ahead of next year's World Test Championship cycle.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

New figures show the number of people refused a blue card, the clearance required to work with children in Queensland, has nearly doubled over the past financial year. The state government says the increase reflects stronger screening processes catching more people who shouldn't be working with children in the first place, pointing to it as evidence the system is doing its job. Legal academics, while broadly supportive of robust screening, have cautioned that a sharp rise in refusals can sometimes bring unintended consequences too, and say it's worth keeping an eye on how appeals and reviews of those decisions are being handled.

Elsewhere, seafood businesses in north Queensland are bracing for the impact of the looming ban on gillnet fishing across the Great Barrier Reef, due to take full effect in 2027. One Ayr seafood shop owner says netted fish make up about eighty per cent of his trade, serving roughly a hundred and fifty families a week, and believes the business probably won't survive once the ban kicks in. Fishers argue that promised alternative fishing gear hasn't materialised in time, and the Queensland Seafood Industry Association is pushing the state and federal governments to consider a longer transition period while that work continues.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The city's major offshore sand dredging program for the year is wrapping up, with the dredge vessel Trud R set to leave Gold Coast waters this week after months of work restoring beaches hit hard by storm erosion. Since April, the operation has pumped roughly one point two million cubic metres of sand back onto the shoreline, with Miami and Mermaid Beach receiving the largest share, bringing the total sand replaced since Tropical Cyclone Alfred to close to three million cubic metres across the whole recovery program.
