In technology, Chinese AI company MiniMax has released a new open-weight image generation model called H3-Image, built on top of the video generation system the company launched earlier this year. The model can produce native four-K resolution images, true sixteen-megapixel output at any aspect ratio, in somewhere between ten and twenty seconds depending on the hardware it's running on, with the company making the full weights freely available for anyone to download and run themselves. Unlike many rival systems that handle text-to-image generation and image editing as separate specialised tools, H3-Image combines both functions into a single unified model, allowing users to both create new images from written prompts and edit existing ones using the same underlying system, including the ability to reference up to sixteen separate source images in a single request. The release continues a broader pattern of Chinese AI labs pushing open-weight models that compete directly with the closed, subscription-based offerings from leading Western developers, often undercutting them significantly on cost while still delivering broadly comparable capability on practical tasks. Independent verification of MiniMax's own performance claims remains limited at this early stage, with the model yet to appear on several of the major third-party benchmarking platforms that typically assess new image generation systems, though early hands-on testing from users who've downloaded the weights has generally been positive about both the image quality and the generation speed on consumer-grade hardware.

Turning to national news, the rollout of the federal government's NDIS reform package continues to progress, with a ban on service providers offering kickbacks and inducements to win new clients set to take effect later this month. The measure forms part of a broader suite of changes stemming from legislation passed by Parliament several months ago, which the government has consistently framed as necessary to put the scheme on a more sustainable long-term financial footing as its overall cost has continued climbing year on year. Further changes are also scheduled to take effect in the weeks ahead, including new claiming timeframes for providers from the start of next month, with additional adjustments to how participant plans roll over not due to commence until early next year. Disability advocacy groups remain split on the broader reform package, with some welcoming measures aimed at curbing poor provider behaviour while others continue to warn that associated funding reductions risk being implemented faster than the sector's capacity to adjust, particularly for participants relying on social and community participation supports that have already seen their allocated budgets reduced this quarter. The government maintains that the changes are being phased in carefully and says it remains committed to consulting with the sector as further elements of the reform package come into force over the coming months.

In Queensland, Brisbane City Council has moved to significantly boost production capacity at its two council-owned quarries, Mount Coot-tha and Bracalba, in order to help meet surging demand for construction material in the lead-up to the 2032 Olympic and Paralympic Games. Council figures suggest the broader south-east Queensland region will need well over thirty million tonnes of rock and aggregate annually to keep pace with its enormous construction pipeline, a figure well beyond current production levels across the region's existing quarries. Bracalba's output has more than doubled over the past decade and is expected to keep expanding, while Mount Coot-tha will continue supplying asphalt aggregate for the Olympic build despite having a more limited remaining reserve of material at the site. Council says the ramp-up is intended to support not just Olympic venues directly but the broader wave of transport, housing, and infrastructure projects already under way across greater Brisbane as the city prepares for a decade of accelerated growth. Longer term planning for the eventual rehabilitation and repurposing of the Mount Coot-tha site, once its remaining reserves are exhausted, remains under separate consideration, with various proposals floated in recent years for transforming the site into a tourism and recreation precinct once quarrying operations there eventually wind down.

And on the Gold Coast, authorities are putting the finishing touches on preparations for this year's Schoolies Week, which will see tens of thousands of school leavers descend on Surfers Paradise for seven days of celebrations starting next week. The Safer Schoolies program will once again anchor the official response, with a dedicated alcohol and drug-free hub set up on Surfers Paradise beach offering water, rest areas, and support services, accessible to registered schoolies wearing official wristbands issued on arrival. A series of temporary road closures will come into effect across the precinct for pedestrian safety during the week's busiest hours, with police and support volunteers stationed throughout the area and a dedicated hotline available around the clock for anyone needing assistance. Local businesses say the week remains one of the most significant events on the Gold Coast's annual calendar, injecting a substantial boost into accommodation, hospitality, and retail trade at a time of year that traditionally marks the unofficial start of the summer tourist season. Organisers say this year's safety planning draws on lessons refined over many previous years of running the event, with authorities expressing confidence the week will again pass with the vast majority of attendees enjoying the celebrations safely and responsibly.
