Good afternoon, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Sunday, the 18th of October, 2026. Here's what you need to know this afternoon, starting with technology and AI.

Governments around the world are taking increasingly different approaches to regulating artificial intelligence, with the United States continuing to favour a largely self-policing model for the industry at the federal level, after officials recently hosted AI executives at the White House and pushed back against efforts to establish a global AI governance framework through the United Nations. Several individual American states are moving in a notably different direction, with California, New York, Illinois, and Maryland all advancing their own AI oversight measures, including a California proposal to fast-track AI auditing requirements and explore the idea of a mandatory emergency shutdown mechanism for high-risk systems. Internationally, the European Union's AI Act already requires developers of the most powerful AI models to report serious incidents to regulators, while China has moved to restrict AI chatbots marketed as virtual companions, particularly where they're accessible to minors. Policy analysts say the widening gap between these approaches means multinational AI companies will increasingly need to navigate a genuinely fragmented global regulatory landscape, rather than a single consistent standard, adding real complexity and cost to operating across multiple major markets simultaneously.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

October is Mental Health Month across the country, with this year's national campaign built around the theme "Together We Thrive" and a renewed focus on community connection following World Mental Health Day earlier this month. The federal government has pointed to a record one point one billion dollars currently committed to strengthening the mental health system, including plans to grow the national network of youth-focused headspace centres from its current one hundred and seventy six locations to more than two hundred by 2030. The government has also highlighted its new Medicare Mental Health Check In service, which gives everyone over the age of sixteen access to a free national mental health screening service for the first time. Mental health advocates have welcomed the continued investment but point to earlier Productivity Commission findings that roughly half a million Australians still aren't getting the specialist mental health care they need, arguing that funding boosts, while welcome, need to translate into genuinely shorter wait times and better access in underserved regional and outer suburban areas specifically.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

A new cargo handling facility at the Port of Townsville is beginning to reshape freight movement across North Queensland, with officials saying the upgrade will reduce the need for long-haul freight trips to other ports further south. The state government says the change should help ease heavy vehicle congestion on sections of the Bruce Highway while also lowering freight costs for businesses that currently have little choice but to truck goods much longer distances to reach adequate port facilities. Industry groups have welcomed the upgrade as a long overdue piece of regional infrastructure, saying North Queensland's freight task has been growing steadily for years without port capacity necessarily keeping pace. Local business groups say the facility should also make Townsville a more attractive base for businesses considering where to locate new freight-dependent operations across the wider region.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

Dreamworld has confirmed plans for a near twenty million dollar new ride called Lost Mine Mayhem, built on the site of its former Motocoaster attraction, with an opening targeted for late 2027. Park management describes the ride as a family adventure attraction designed to appeal to a broad range of ages, forming part of the park's ongoing effort to refresh its ride lineup after a string of new investments in recent years. Theme park analysts say sustained reinvestment of this kind is essential for Gold Coast parks competing for the same pool of domestic and international visitors, particularly as newer attractions elsewhere in the country and overseas continue raising the bar for what families expect from a major theme park day out. Local tourism operators say a strong line-up of fresh, well-promoted new rides across the city's theme parks tends to benefit the broader Gold Coast visitor economy too, not just the individual parks themselves, by giving holidaymakers another reason to extend their stay in the region.
