In technology, French AI company Mistral has been pushing ahead with the rollout of its latest flagship model, Mistral Large 4, after opening a public preview of the system last month and progressively releasing more of its underlying architecture since then. The model uses a mixture-of-experts design with roughly one trillion total parameters, though only a fraction of those are active for any given task, an approach the company says allows it to deliver strong performance without the full computing cost of running every parameter simultaneously. Mistral has positioned the new model as a genuine rival to the leading American labs' flagship systems, and it's drawn particular attention for its strength on cybersecurity-related benchmarks, with the company saying it can handle legitimate defensive security tasks that many competing models tend to refuse or hedge on. The model also accepts both text and images as input, allowing it to handle tasks like analysing documents or diagrams alongside ordinary text prompts. Independent analysts say Mistral's continued push to remain competitive with the much larger American AI labs, despite a fraction of their funding and computing resources, reflects a broader push within Europe to maintain at least one credible homegrown alternative in a field increasingly dominated by a handful of well-resourced American and Chinese companies.

Turning to national news, the Reserve Bank's latest statement on monetary policy has flagged that inflation remains well above target, with the central bank pointing to a number of upside risks that have emerged since its previous assessment in August. The Reserve Bank lifted the cash rate again at its November meeting, taking it to four-point-eight-five per cent, in what was already its fourth increase this year as it continues trying to bring price growth back under control. In its latest statement, the bank attributed some of the renewed inflationary pressure to global cost increases linked to ongoing tension in the Middle East, as well as surging demand for computing power and energy tied to the broader artificial intelligence boom, both of which it says have pushed up costs in ways that are proving difficult to fully offset domestically. The bank's commentary suggests policymakers are increasingly having to factor global, structural forces well beyond domestic control into their outlook, rather than relying solely on traditional levers like interest rates to manage price pressures at home. Economists say the acknowledgement of AI-related cost pressures as a genuine driver of inflation marks a notable shift in how seriously central banks are now treating the economic footprint of the technology boom, beyond just its effects on stock markets and employment.

In Queensland, the state's push to position itself as a major destination for international sport continues, with Rugby Australia having confirmed earlier this year that Townsville will host a further three Wallabies test matches over the coming seasons, building on the city's growing reputation as a genuine events hub for North Queensland. The agreement locks in Test opponents across the next several years, continuing a partnership between Rugby Australia and the state government that's been credited with delivering a significant boost to tourism, hospitality, and the broader regional economy each time a major international match comes to town. Local tourism operators say these kinds of marquee sporting fixtures draw visitors from across the country and overseas alike, filling hotels and restaurants well beyond what a typical weekend would normally bring to the region. State officials say the strategy of spreading major sporting fixtures beyond the traditional Brisbane and Gold Coast venues has been a deliberate one, aimed at ensuring regional Queensland communities share more directly in the economic benefits that come with hosting world-class sport.
