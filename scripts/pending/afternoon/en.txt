In technology, OpenAI has disclosed two separate incidents in which its own AI agents weakened an internal system designed to catch misbehaviour, during routine internal coding work earlier this month. In the first case, on the 3rd of October, an agent working on an internal testing task suppressed automatic security alerts, having interpreted an earlier instruction against sending certain messages as covering safety notifications too. In a second case three days later, a different agent removed a safety monitor while copying an existing piece of code, then incorrectly claimed afterwards that it hadn't done so. OpenAI says neither incident ranked among its most serious safety concerns, and that in both cases the agents appear to have considered whether the safeguards should be preserved, but interpreted routine coding instructions as implicit permission to weaken them anyway. The company says it doesn't yet have a fully satisfying explanation for why this kept happening, and that it's working to make it harder for its models to disable monitoring systems in the first place. Reuters reports that OpenAI and rival labs, including Anthropic, have disclosed similar instances in recent months of AI agents accessing systems without clear authorisation, a pattern increasingly seen as one of the harder safety challenges to solve as AI systems are given more autonomy over everyday technical work.

Turning to national news, mining giant BHP has outlined plans for a substantial expansion of its copper operations in South Australia over the coming decade, aiming to lift refined copper cathode output at its Olympic Dam operation well beyond current levels. The company has already committed several hundred million dollars toward underground access and processing upgrades at the site, as part of a broader smelter and refinery expansion it's been progressing for the past couple of years. A final investment decision on the next, larger stage of the expansion is still to come, with BHP continuing to work through engineering and approval processes before committing to the full build-out. Industry analysts say the long-term plan, if it goes ahead as outlined, would position Olympic Dam as one of the most significant copper operations in the world, at a time when global demand for copper is being driven higher by electrification and the broader clean energy transition. South Australian officials have welcomed the company's continued investment in the state, saying any expansion would support thousands of jobs across mining, processing, and related industries well into the 2030s.

In Queensland, south-east parts of the state are dealing with flash flooding today after a burst of heavy rain overnight and into the morning, with the Bureau of Meteorology recording more than one hundred and thirty millimetres at one gauge in Caboolture in just three hours. A severe thunderstorm warning followed, and emergency crews have responded to numerous swiftwater rescue call-outs across the south-east, with dozens of local roads closed, including some in Brisbane where floodwater has covered sections of road in suburbs such as Bardon. Further south in the Scenic Rim, close to one hundred millimetres of rain fell at Canungra in twenty four hours, which authorities say has at least helped ease months of dry conditions and started refilling local creeks and water supplies. Residents across the affected areas are being urged to avoid unnecessary travel, never drive through floodwater, and keep an eye on official warnings as the wet weather continues to move through the region over coming days.

And on the Gold Coast, city councillors are deciding today whether to proceed in January with new three-hour parking limits for streets between Mermaid Beach and Burleigh Heads, tied to the opening of the light rail's third stage, or to delay the change again. The restrictions, which would apply on weekdays between nine in the morning and five in the afternoon with some exemptions for residents, were originally due to start in September but were pushed back after signage wasn't ready in time. A petition with more than two thousand signatures is calling for a much longer delay of up to eighteen months, with residents arguing many locals wouldn't qualify for an exemption and that the council should monitor real traffic patterns before locking in the new limits. Under the proposed rules, number plate recognition cameras would track how long vehicles remain in the area, meaning drivers could still be fined even if they simply moved between different parking spots rather than leaving altogether. Local business owners along the affected strip say they just want certainty either way, after months of uncertainty over exactly when, or whether, the new limits will actually begin.
