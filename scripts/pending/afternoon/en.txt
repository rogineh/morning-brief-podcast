Good afternoon, and welcome to your Afternoon Edition for Thursday, the 5th of November, 2026. Here's what's been making news today in technology, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, Apple has cut the daily budget some of its own engineers are allowed to spend on internal artificial intelligence tools, reducing the cap from three hundred dollars down to two hundred dollars a day, with reports suggesting further reductions to as little as one hundred dollars could follow for some engineering teams. Employees outside engineering roles are reportedly facing even tighter restrictions, with the company said to be weighing a daily limit as low as twenty-five dollars for general staff using AI tools in their day-to-day work. Interestingly, some staff have noted that relatively few workers were actually using their full previous allowance anyway, meaning the real-world savings from the change may end up considerably smaller than the headline cut suggests. The move comes under the direction of a senior Apple executive pushing for a leaner overall operation, even as the company's own research and development spending has climbed substantially this year, driven largely by continued investment in AI and supporting infrastructure. Some staff have reportedly interpreted the tightened budgets as an early signal of broader cost discipline to come, particularly following job cuts affecting several hundred positions earlier this year, though Apple itself hasn't publicly confirmed the specific figures being reported.

Turning to national news, department store chain David Jones has collapsed into voluntary administration today, marking the first time in its hundred and eighty-eight year history that the retailer has fallen into this kind of formal insolvency process. The move follows a breakdown in negotiations with Scentre Group, the company's largest landlord, which hosts roughly seventeen of David Jones' thirty-eight stores, after the retailer reportedly fell behind on rental payments across its network. Administrators from one firm and receivers from another have now been appointed, and will immediately begin searching for buyers interested in either recapitalising the business or purchasing it outright as an ongoing operation rather than liquidating it piece by piece. Stores remain open and trading normally for now while administrators review the business in detail, though more than six thousand staff across Australia and New Zealand face considerable uncertainty about their jobs in the months ahead. Consumer advocates are specifically warning shoppers holding gift cards or considering purchases that depend on future returns or exchanges to act relatively quickly, cautioning that there's no guarantee the department store chain will still be operating in its current form come the new year.

In Queensland, authorities in Mackay have used newly introduced local police powers for the first time this week, banning a man from the city's central business district after he was found carrying a knife inside a shopping centre. Mackay is now one of only two locations across Queensland where police hold these particular powers, allowing officers to remove and formally ban individuals from a designated city area for up to a month in response to threatening, disorderly, or violent behaviour. The powers apply specifically within a defined zone covering the main shopping centre, the city heart, and a central public park, and sit alongside existing laws already allowing police across the state to scan members of the public for concealed knives without a warrant in any public place. A police superintendent overseeing the Mackay-Whitsunday region said officers would maintain an increased visible presence across the designated city area going forward, following data showing thousands of people have already been scanned for weapons across the wider region over the past year. The new local banning powers add an additional tool for police specifically targeting repeat troublemakers in the city centre, on top of the broader weapon-detection laws already operating statewide.

And on the Gold Coast, the city's long-running push to secure a major new indoor arena faces a critical juncture this month, with the mayor preparing to meet Queensland's deputy premier to discuss how to close a funding gap now estimated at around four hundred million dollars. Projected costs for the proposed venue have climbed to roughly a billion dollars in total, well above earlier estimates, prompting the council to pause active planning while it works out how the shortfall might realistically be bridged. Rather than requesting a conventional direct cash contribution from the state government, the mayor is instead proposing a land-based solution, exploring how dormant state-owned land parcels might be used to help unlock the value needed to get construction moving. A dedicated city investment body has been preparing a detailed feasibility and funding model specifically to present during this month's meeting, aiming to demonstrate a credible pathway for the project despite its ballooning price tag. Even if this month's talks produce an in-principle agreement on how state land might contribute to the project, the council and its development partner would still need to finalise a viable overall financial model and negotiate a formal development agreement before construction could realistically begin.
