Turning to national news, the Department of Veterans' Affairs says major reforms to the veteran support system have delivered dramatic improvements in how quickly claims are processed, with average allocation times for initial liability claims falling to around two weeks, down from well over three hundred days just a few years ago. The improvement follows legislative changes that moved new compensation and rehabilitation claims onto a single, streamlined act from the first of July, simplifying a system that had long been criticised for forcing veterans to navigate multiple overlapping pieces of legislation depending on when and how they'd served. Travel reimbursements for veterans have also sped up dramatically, now typically processed within about four days, compared with a fortnight or more under the previous arrangements, while other claim categories have likewise seen their average processing times cut substantially. Veterans' advocacy groups have broadly welcomed the changes, saying lengthy claims delays have been a longstanding source of frustration and genuine hardship for former service members waiting on support they're entitled to, particularly those dealing with service-related injuries or illnesses that require urgent treatment. The department says it will continue publishing detailed claims processing data on a regular basis, arguing that greater transparency around performance helps hold the system accountable and gives veterans a clearer sense of what to expect when they lodge a claim.

In Queensland, authorities have completed the first project under the state's five-hundred-million-dollar Beef Roads Program, with a three-kilometre stretch of the Clermont to Alpha Road in Central Queensland now finished and open to cattle transport traffic. The broader program, jointly funded by the federal and state governments, is designed to upgrade dozens of unsealed and ageing roads across the state's key cattle-producing regions, addressing long-standing complaints from beef producers that poor-quality roads add significant extra cost and risk to getting cattle to market. Further projects under the program remain underway elsewhere in the region, including sections further south along the same corridor and on other key freight routes used heavily by the beef industry, with the broader rollout of two dozen projects expected to continue incrementally over the coming decade. Industry groups say upgrading these routes delivers tangible benefits well beyond the beef sector itself, given the same roads are also relied on by other agricultural freight, emergency services, and regional communities that have often gone without significant road investment for years. Transport officials say completing this first section on schedule provides a solid proof of concept for the rest of the program, with lessons learned from the early works package expected to help streamline delivery of the remaining projects still to come.

And on the Gold Coast, Griffith University has continued building its international academic reputation, having climbed nearly forty places in this year's global university rankings to sit inside the top two hundred and thirty universities worldwide. University leaders say the improvement reflects sustained investment in research output and growing international recognition of programs based across its Gold Coast and Brisbane campuses, with the Gold Coast campus in particular playing an increasingly prominent role in the university's research profile in areas like health, environmental science, and engineering. The ranking improvement comes as the university continues expanding its connections to major local events and infrastructure projects, including research partnerships tied to preparations for the twenty thirty-two Olympic and Paralympic Games. Local education sector figures say a strengthening international reputation helps the broader region attract more international students and research funding, both of which flow through into the wider Gold Coast economy via accommodation, retail, and hospitality spending from the university's growing student population. University administrators say they expect the campus's research and teaching profile to continue growing in the years ahead, pointing to ongoing investment in new facilities and continued recruitment of research staff across a range of disciplines.
