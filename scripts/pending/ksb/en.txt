Turning to national news, Australia's corporate regulator has issued what it's calling a final warning to financial advice licensees who rely on lead generators to drum up superannuation switching business, saying the practice may now put those firms' licences at risk. The regulator's review found some advisers were using high-pressure cold calls and misleading social media advertising to push people toward switching their super funds, often relying on fear-based claims, exaggerated promises of better returns, and comparisons that weren't truly like for like. According to the findings, lead generators referred thousands of potential clients to advisers over the past eighteen months, with more than six hundred million dollars in superannuation moved as a result of those referrals. The regulator says warning signs include being contacted out of the blue, pressure to act immediately, claims that your current fund is underperforming, and offers of a free super health check or help tracking down lost super. Its advice to consumers who find themselves on one of these calls is simple: just hang up, block the number, and consider joining the Do Not Call Register. The Assistant Treasurer has also flagged plans to ban unlicensed telemarketers from cold-calling people about switching their superannuation altogether, as authorities look to close off the practice at its source rather than simply policing it after the fact.

In Queensland, the state and federal governments have confirmed that planning and design work is progressing on a thirty-six million dollar project to deliver long-term water security for the remote community of Woorabinda, south-west of Rockhampton. The project builds on urgent repairs carried out at the town's water treatment plant, which brought an end to an eleven-month boil water alert by giving operators better tools to monitor water quality and adjust treatment as conditions in the raw water supply change. The longer-term plan centres on a new water treatment plant that will reuse parts of the upgraded existing facility, with the aim of reliably meeting the Australian Drinking Water Guidelines for the community well into the future. Funding for the project is split between the two levels of government, drawing on a federal water infrastructure program alongside a dedicated state fund aimed at closing the gap in outcomes between Indigenous and non-Indigenous Australians. Authorities say the project reflects a broader push to ensure remote and regional communities aren't left behind when it comes to basic services that are often taken for granted in the cities, with clean, reliable drinking water considered a fundamental foundation for community health and wellbeing.

And on the Gold Coast, fresh regional labour force figures show the local economy continuing to outperform much of the rest of the state, with the Gold Coast's unemployment rate sitting well below the Queensland average and ranking among the lowest of the state's nineteen statistical regions. The latest data points to employment approaching four hundred thousand people across the region, with solid growth recorded over the past year even as the workforce participation rate eased slightly. Economists tracking the figures say the result reflects the Gold Coast's increasingly diverse economic base, with construction, healthcare, tourism, and hospitality all contributing to steady jobs growth, alongside continued population growth drawing more workers and businesses to the region. Local business groups have welcomed the figures, saying a tight labour market shows ongoing confidence in the city's economy even as some employers report difficulty finding workers in certain sectors. The Queensland Government Statistician's Office, which compiles the figures from Australian Bureau of Statistics data using a rolling twelve-month average to smooth out seasonal swings, says the broader trend across the Gold Coast has remained consistently strong for some time, with the region regularly posting some of the healthiest jobs figures of any part of the state.
