Now to Queensland. Emergency dredging work is underway at Moore Park Beach, around four hundred kilometres north of Brisbane, after the coastline lost roughly five metres of foreshore since Tropical Cyclone Alfred struck last year, with a major flood event earlier this year making the erosion considerably worse. Crews are pumping extra sand onto the beach in the hope of stabilising it before the busy Christmas period, when safety fencing currently blocking off the worst-affected stretch is expected to come down. Coastal researchers have welcomed the immediate fix but say councils up and down the Queensland coast need to start planning for longer-term solutions, warning that dredging alone won't be enough if storms and flooding continue to strip away sand faster than it can be replaced. The situation at Moore Park is being watched closely by other coastal communities facing similar erosion pressures as extreme weather events become more frequent. Local business owners say the damage has already affected tourism over the past year, with some caravan parks and holiday rentals losing direct beach access, and they're hoping the current works will at least buy enough time for a more permanent plan to be developed before the next major storm season arrives.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. Southport's much-loved volunteer marine rescue crew has begun a new chapter, transitioning from Volunteer Marine Rescue Southport into Marine Rescue Queensland as part of a state-wide unification of marine rescue services. The change caps off more than sixty years of service on the water dating back to 1969, and the incoming unit commander says the spirit behind the operation isn't going anywhere, describing marine rescue on the Gold Coast as, in his words, the story of ordinary people doing extraordinary things for others. Many of the volunteers who built the organisation's reputation over the decades have also set up a new independent support group to keep fundraising and community ties strong even as the operational side moves under the new statewide banner. Locals on the water can expect the same familiar faces and the same rapid response the crew has long been known for, just under a new name and with the backing of a larger, better-resourced state network behind them. Boaties across the Broadwater and surrounding waterways have been encouraged to update their emergency contact details to reflect the new branding, with the crew stressing that radio channels, call-out procedures, and phone numbers used to request help on the water remain unchanged during the transition.
