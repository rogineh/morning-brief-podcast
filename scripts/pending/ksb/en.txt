Starting with national news, a new emergency warning system called AusAlert has begun rolling out across the country, designed to replace the existing location-targeted, SMS-based alert system that's been in place for years. The new cell-broadcast technology pushes warnings directly to every compatible phone in a defined area almost instantly, rather than relying on a text message queue that can sometimes lag behind a fast-moving emergency. Authorities say the switch should mean faster, more reliable alerts during floods, bushfires, and other emergencies, and they're encouraging people to make sure their phone software is up to date so they don't miss out on the improved system. The technology is already in use in several other countries and has been credited with cutting the time it takes to reach large numbers of people during fast-moving disasters.

Elsewhere, housing remains firmly in the national conversation, with this year's federal budget setting aside just over three billion dollars specifically to help build another hundred thousand new homes across the country. Housing advocates have broadly welcomed the funding as a step in the right direction, though many continue to argue that construction capacity, land supply, and approval delays, rather than funding alone, remain the biggest barriers to actually getting new housing built at the pace needed to ease the current affordability crunch. Industry groups say skilled labour shortages in particular will need to be addressed if the target is to be met within any reasonable timeframe.

Turning now to Queensland, Rockhampton Regional Council has started work on a one point eight million dollar conservation project aimed at protecting the city's heritage-listed Customs House, one of the region's most recognisable historic buildings. The project is expected to address structural issues that have built up over decades, with council officials saying the goal is to safeguard the building for future generations while keeping it usable as a venue for community events and functions in the years ahead. Customs House has stood in the city for well over a century, and locals have long regarded it as one of the most distinctive landmarks along the Fitzroy River.

Also in Queensland, the state government has opened a new round of expressions of interest under its Destination 2045 tourism strategy, inviting operators to put forward ideas for the next batch of regional ecotourism experiences to be developed around the state. The initiative already has several projects identified in North Queensland, and tourism officials say the goal is to spread visitor dollars more evenly beyond the state's already well-established hotspots, giving lesser-known regional areas a stronger foothold in the tourism market. Successful applicants will reportedly receive support to help get their projects investment-ready, rather than funding for construction itself, with officials hoping that groundwork will make it easier to attract private backers down the track.

And here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

New figures confirm what plenty of locals have long suspected, the Gold Coast is growing faster than greater Brisbane and has outpaced the average growth rate of Australian capital cities for several years running. Recent analysis shows the region has added close to seventy thousand new residents over the past five years alone, with growth running at close to double the national average. Council planners say more than half of that growth in the wider south-east region is actually happening outside Brisbane itself, reinforcing the Gold Coast's position as one of the fastest-growing parts of the country, even as some local councillors warn that infrastructure investment will need to keep pace to avoid the city's roads, schools, and services becoming overwhelmed.

Looking further ahead, city planners say the Gold Coast's population is on track to reach one million residents within the next two decades, a milestone that's already shaping long-term decisions around transport corridors, water infrastructure, and future housing supply across the region. Officials say getting those foundations right now is seen as critical to making sure the city's growth stays manageable rather than becoming a source of strain further down the track.
