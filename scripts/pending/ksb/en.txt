Police forces across the country are rapidly expanding their use of drones, with Western Australia Police launching what's being described as an Australian first in automated policing technology. Rather than deploying officer-flown drones on an ad hoc basis, the new system uses aircraft stationed in dedicated docking facilities across Perth's southern suburbs, which can be launched and operated remotely from a command centre in the city. The state's Police Minister has described the capability as a genuine first for the country, with officials saying the drones can reach the scene of an incident well ahead of officers travelling by car in many cases, potentially giving commanders real-time information before anyone arrives on foot. New South Wales Police have been running a broadly similar remote drone trial out of Moree, piloted from a dedicated operations centre near Sydney, which the state government has already moved to expand after surveys reportedly showed strong community support for the approach. Civil liberties groups have raised concerns that this kind of rapid technological rollout risks outpacing the privacy and oversight frameworks meant to keep pace with new surveillance capabilities, particularly given some of the broader drone programs have also been linked to separate facial recognition trials occurring elsewhere in the country.

In Queensland, dairy company Bega has struck a ten-year deal to back a new cold storage facility at Edmonton, near Cairns, aimed at giving the region a much-needed buffer against future flooding and severe weather disruptions. The company's chief executive said Bega has previously had to truck milk all the way to Brisbane for storage when local capacity ran out, a costly and inefficient workaround the new facility is specifically designed to eliminate. The planned site will offer roughly half again the storage capacity of Bega's existing Malanda facility, giving the company much greater flexibility to hold surplus or seasonal milk locally rather than relying on distant storage during periods of high production or supply disruption. Construction on the new facility is expected to get underway next year, with local dairy farmers likely to benefit from reduced transport costs and a more resilient local supply chain capable of weathering future flood events without the same scramble for emergency storage space seen in past wet seasons. The investment reflects a broader pattern of agricultural businesses in flood-prone regional areas building in additional redundancy and local capacity, rather than assuming distant storage and transport networks will always remain reliably accessible when severe weather strikes.

And on the Gold Coast, the city is hosting the Asia Pacific Screen Awards and Forum from today, bringing filmmakers, industry figures, and film lovers from across the region together for three days of screenings, panel discussions, and an awards ceremony celebrating the best in Asia-Pacific cinema. Today's opening night features a special tribute screening at Pacific Fair, introduced by veteran Australian actor Jack Thompson, who currently serves as president of the awards' academy and will be honoured as part of the evening's program. Forum sessions exploring the business and craft of filmmaking will run across several Gold Coast venues over the coming days, before the awards ceremony itself wraps up proceedings on Friday evening at one of the city's major entertainment venues. Organisers say the event, now in its nineteenth year for the awards and eighth year for the accompanying forum, continues to grow as a genuine drawcard for the regional film industry, attracting delegates and media attention from across the Asia-Pacific region to the Gold Coast each year. Local tourism and hospitality operators say events of this kind provide a welcome, high-value visitor boost during a period of the year that can otherwise fall between the city's bigger annual drawcards.
