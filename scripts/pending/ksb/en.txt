Changes to National Disability Insurance Scheme funding have begun taking effect this month, with budgets for certain categories of support reduced as part of a broader push by the federal government to slow the scheme's rapid cost growth. Support for social, economic, and community participation is being cut by around half, while funding for improved daily living skills supports has been reduced by a smaller margin, with the changes rolling out gradually over the next year as individual participants reach their regular plan reassessment or renewal date rather than applying to everyone all at once. The government says existing participants will keep their current plan and funding levels until that reassessment point, meaning nobody faces an immediate, unexpected cut to support already locked in. Disability advocates have raised concerns that the reductions could meaningfully affect participants' ability to stay socially connected and active in their communities, while the government maintains the scheme's underlying guarantees remain intact and that targeted adjustments are necessary to keep the NDIS financially sustainable over the long term. Some disability support providers say they're already fielding anxious questions from clients worried about what the changes will mean for them specifically, and are urging participants to seek clear advice on their own reassessment date rather than assuming the cuts apply immediately across the board.

In Queensland, the state's health department has been working through the findings of a first-of-its-kind survey of its medical workforce's wellbeing, with officials saying the results will directly shape future support programs for frontline clinicians. The survey sought to better understand the pressures facing doctors and other medical staff across the public hospital system, with workforce fatigue and burnout among the specific issues officials say they're now looking to address more systematically. Health department representatives say the goal is to move toward more proactive, preventative support for clinical staff, rather than only responding once a doctor or nurse is already in crisis or close to leaving the profession altogether. Medical workforce groups have welcomed the initiative, saying staff wellbeing has a direct flow-on effect on patient care quality, and that a health system struggling to retain experienced clinicians ultimately struggles to deliver consistently good outcomes for the patients it serves. Officials say further detail on specific new support measures stemming from the survey will be released in coming months, with regional and rural facilities expected to be a particular focus given the additional pressures clinicians in those areas often face compared with their metropolitan counterparts.

Meanwhile on the Gold Coast, planning is progressing on a light rail spur extension linking the Gold Coast University Hospital precinct to Biggera Waters, with three new stations proposed along the roughly three kilometre route. The extension forms part of a broader state transport plan for the city released earlier this year, which also includes new metro-style bus services elsewhere in the region following the cancellation of a separate, more controversial light rail extension toward the airport. Transport planners say connecting the hospital precinct more directly to northern suburbs like Labrador and Biggera Waters would improve access for patients, staff, and visitors who currently rely heavily on cars or infrequent bus services to reach the site. Local residents in the areas along the proposed route have given a mixed early reaction, with some welcoming improved public transport access and others raising questions about construction disruption and the project's eventual cost and funding source. The government has not yet released a firm construction timeline or total cost estimate for the spur, saying more detailed planning work needs to be completed first before either figure can be confirmed with confidence.
