In national news, a new analysis has found Australia has paid the heaviest price of any country in the world during this year's global diesel supply crunch, with the nation's extra import costs estimated at around nine billion dollars over the past six months alone. The shortage has been driven largely by disruption tied to conflict in the Middle East, which has squeezed global refining capacity and pushed diesel prices up far more sharply than petrol over the course of the year, with some analysts putting the additional cost to the average household at roughly five hundred dollars. Australia's heavy reliance on imported diesel has left it particularly exposed, with the country accounting for a substantial share of the world's seaborne diesel trade and holding only a few weeks of reserves in the tank at any given time. Freight and transport industry groups say the sustained price pressure has hit the logistics sector especially hard, given how central diesel is to trucking, rail freight, and agriculture, with some operators warning that margins have been squeezed to breaking point in recent months. There's been a little relief at the bowser more broadly, with unleaded petrol prices easing slightly over the past month as wholesale costs have softened, even as diesel prices have remained stubbornly elevated and shown few signs of a swift return to more typical levels.

In Queensland, the state government has unveiled its preferred design for a major upgrade to the Bribie Island Bridge, confirming plans for an entirely new four-lane traffic bridge to be built just north of the existing crossing. Under the proposal, the current bridge would be retained but repurposed as a dedicated pedestrian, cycling, and mobility-device link, preserving it as a lifestyle connection to the island even once the new structure takes over as the main traffic route. The project is expected to cost in the order of seven hundred and fifty million dollars once complete, reflecting the scale of works involved in both the new bridge itself and improvements to surrounding approach roads and intersections. Community consultation on the design is open now and continues until the end of this month, giving residents, businesses, and other stakeholders a chance to weigh in before the project moves further into detailed planning. Transport authorities say the existing bridge has long been a chokepoint for the island's roughly twenty thousand residents, particularly during peak holiday periods when traffic volumes climb sharply, and that a four-lane replacement is intended to meaningfully ease congestion for decades to come as the broader region continues to grow.

And on the Gold Coast, authorities have continued expanding a drone-based shark monitoring program along the coastline, with new patrol locations added to the network in recent months as part of a broader push to make the technology a permanent fixture of beach safety across the state. The program now operates in close partnership with Surf Life Saving Queensland, running at a growing number of Gold Coast beaches alongside traditional lifeguard patrols, with drone operators able to spot sharks and other hazards from the air and alert lifesavers on the ground in real time. State authorities have committed to continuing the expansion heading into the warmer months, with the network expected to grow further across the coming season as part of a broader strategy that blends aerial monitoring with existing tagging and netting measures already in place at popular swimming beaches. Lifesaving officials say the drones have proven particularly useful during busy holiday periods and weekends, when beaches are at their most crowded and the value of an early aerial warning is at its highest, giving swimmers and surfers additional peace of mind without changing how they use the water day to day. The expansion comes as beach safety authorities prepare for what's expected to be another busy summer season of visitors flocking to the Gold Coast's stretch of coastline.
