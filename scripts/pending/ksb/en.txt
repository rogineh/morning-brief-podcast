New figures confirm that real wages have now fallen for a third consecutive quarter, with the Wage Price Index rising three point two per cent over the past year while inflation ran hotter at three point seven per cent over the same period, meaning the typical pay packet is buying less than it was twelve months ago. The squeeze comes despite the Fair Work Commission lifting the minimum wage by four point seven five per cent from the first of July, a decision the Commission itself described as unable to deliver a genuine real increase given how uncertain economic conditions remain. Economists say the gap between wage growth and inflation has become one of the more persistent features of the post-pandemic economy, with workers in a number of industries reporting that pay rises secured through enterprise agreements are struggling to keep pace with the actual cost of groceries, rent, and energy bills. Unions argue the figures strengthen the case for further wage increases in the next bargaining round, while some employer groups warn that pushing wages up faster than productivity growth risks adding further fuel to inflation rather than easing the underlying pressure on households. Household budget surveys conducted over the past few months suggest many families have responded by cutting back on discretionary spending such as dining out and entertainment, rather than falling behind on essentials like housing and utility bills, though financial counsellors say that trade-off is becoming harder to sustain the longer the real wage squeeze continues.

In Queensland, construction is progressing on what's being described as the state's largest combined solar and battery project, located near Townsville and backed through the federal government's Capacity Investment Scheme. The co-located solar and storage facility is targeting full operation by 2028, and proponents say it will add significant firming capacity to the grid, helping smooth out the kind of supply swings that can occur as more intermittent renewable generation comes online across the state. Regional business groups have welcomed the investment as a boost to construction jobs in North Queensland in the near term, while energy analysts say large-scale battery storage projects like this one will be increasingly critical to keeping the lights on reliably as coal-fired generation continues to retire over the coming decade. The federal scheme underpinning the investment is designed to give developers enough revenue certainty to commit to large storage projects before they'd otherwise be commercially viable on their own, with officials arguing that approach is cheaper in the long run than leaving the market to solve the reliability challenge unassisted.

Meanwhile on the Gold Coast, new labour market data shows the city's employers are still struggling to fill a significant share of advertised roles, even as the overall volume of job advertisements has eased slightly compared with the same time last year. More than a third of surveyed employers reported difficulty recruiting over the past year, with a shortage of suitably experienced applicants cited as the most common obstacle. Health care and social assistance remains comfortably the city's largest employing industry, while professional, scientific, and technical services is forecast to be among the fastest growing sectors for new jobs in the years ahead. Local business groups say addressing the skills shortage will likely require a combination of more local training pathways and continued efforts to attract experienced workers from elsewhere in the country, particularly as major infrastructure projects tied to the lead-up to the 2032 Olympics continue to ramp up demand for construction and engineering skills specifically. Recruitment agencies say they're also seeing stronger than usual demand in allied health and aged care roles, reflecting the same broader population growth and ageing trends playing out across the rest of the state, and some are urging local training providers to expand intake in those specific courses rather than relying solely on interstate migration to fill the gap.
