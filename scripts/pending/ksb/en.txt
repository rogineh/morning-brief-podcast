In Queensland, the latest police crime statistics show a continued decline in assault offences across the state, with the force's public dashboard recording a drop of close to eight per cent compared with the same period last year. The figures form part of a broader pattern police have been highlighting in recent months, with earlier data released in July also showing a reduction in the number of crime victims following a sustained push around intelligence-led policing and targeted operations in known hotspot areas. State government figures released earlier this year, drawing on separate Bureau of Statistics data, showed total victim numbers down close to ten per cent against pre-pandemic levels, even as some independent analysts have cautioned that the picture varies considerably from one offence category and one region to another. Police have attributed the overall trend to a combination of expanded patrol operations, tougher sentencing settings introduced over the past couple of years, and improved intelligence sharing between different policing units across the state. Community safety advocates have broadly welcomed the continued downward trend, while also cautioning that sustained, consistent investment in prevention and support services will be just as important as enforcement in keeping the numbers moving in the right direction over the longer term. Police say they'll continue publishing updated figures through their public crime statistics dashboard, allowing residents in individual local government areas to track trends specific to their own neighbourhood rather than relying solely on the statewide averages.

And on the Gold Coast, the city council has formally adopted a new Local Disaster Management Plan, broadening the scope of risks the city formally prepares for beyond traditional natural disasters like floods and bushfires to also include cyber attacks and terrorism. The updated plan reflects a growing recognition among local governments that disaster preparedness increasingly needs to account for a wider range of disruptive events, not just the storms and flooding the Gold Coast has traditionally focused most of its planning around. Council officials say the plan sets out clearer lines of responsibility and coordination between different council departments, state agencies, and emergency services in the event of a major disruption, whatever form that disruption ultimately takes. The move comes as the council separately continues its ongoing coastal protection work, with recent dredging operations having added a meaningful volume of sand along the city's beachfront between Miami and Main Beach, helping bolster natural storm defences ahead of the wetter months still to come. Local emergency management groups have welcomed the plan's broader scope, saying modern disaster planning simply has to keep pace with the full range of threats a city like the Gold Coast can realistically face in any given year. The council says it will continue reviewing and updating the plan periodically, rather than treating today's adoption as a final, unchanging document, so that it can keep adapting as new types of risk emerge over time.
