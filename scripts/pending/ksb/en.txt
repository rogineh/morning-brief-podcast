The federal and Tasmanian governments have secured the future of the Bell Bay Aluminium smelter through to the end of 2031, backing a two hundred million dollar support package alongside a new five year electricity agreement between the smelter's operator and the state's power provider. The deal resolves a dispute over an expiring power contract that had put the smelter's future in doubt, and officials say it directly protects around five hundred and fifty jobs at the plant, plus roughly a thousand more across northern Tasmania that depend on it indirectly. The smelter, which is Australia's oldest and among its most energy-efficient, uses more than a quarter of the state's entire electricity supply, making the new power agreement just as critical to the deal as the direct funding support itself. The parties had originally been negotiating for a ten year arrangement, and the federal minister responsible says work will continue on a longer-term plan for the region even with this shorter-term deal now locked in. Union representatives at the smelter welcomed the announcement as a significant relief for workers and their families, many of whom had faced genuine uncertainty over the plant's future in the lead-up to the previous contract's expiry, while business groups in northern Tasmania said the deal removes a major cloud that had been hanging over the regional economy for months.

In Queensland, construction continues to progress on the Cross River Rail project, with significant work now visible at the new underground Albert Street station in the Brisbane CBD, including the station's distinctive canopy structure and what will be the state's longest escalator. The project links the Gabba and the central business district with a new river crossing, after crews completed the tunnelling beneath the Brisbane River in earlier stages of construction. Transport officials say the project remains one of the most complex pieces of infrastructure ever built in the state, and that reaching this visible, above-ground stage of construction at Albert Street marks a significant milestone after years of underground tunnelling work that was largely invisible to the public. The government says further updates on the project's remaining construction timeline will be provided as the next stages of station fit-out get underway. Commuters who've watched the project's lengthy construction disrupt parts of the inner city for years say they're looking forward to finally seeing tangible progress above ground, after a long stretch in which most of the project's work was happening well out of public view beneath the city's streets and riverbed.

Meanwhile on the Gold Coast, the council has officially opened the first stage of the Greenheart Robina Parklands, a twenty two and a half hectare green space that forms part of what's planned to become the city's largest ever parkland project. More than twenty five million dollars has been invested in this opening stage alone, which includes new playgrounds, picnic areas, walking paths, open sporting fields, and around four hundred newly planted trees. The council has also set aside funding in its latest budget to begin concept design work on the broader Greenheart project, which has already received federal environmental approval, with public submissions on the wider plan now being assessed. Local residents have welcomed the new green space as a significant addition to the city's recreational facilities, particularly in a rapidly growing part of the Gold Coast where access to large open parkland has previously lagged behind new housing development. Council representatives say the staged approach to delivering the broader Greenheart vision allows residents to start enjoying parts of the space well before the entire project is finished, rather than waiting years for a single, all-at-once opening once every stage of planning and construction is complete.
