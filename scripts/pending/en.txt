In technology, Chinese AI company DeepSeek has closed its second major funding round, raising somewhere in the region of fifteen to sixteen billion dollars according to multiple reports, with the exact figure still not officially confirmed by the company itself. The round was backed heavily by Tencent and Contemporary Amperex Technology, better known as CATL and the world's largest electric vehicle battery maker, with one report putting DeepSeek's valuation heading into the round's final close at roughly seventy four billion dollars. The raise is reportedly more than double what DeepSeek had originally been targeting, and is widely seen as laying the groundwork for a possible initial public offering as early as 2027, with the fresh capital expected to go toward building out AI data centres and continuing development of the company's open-source AI models. DeepSeek rose to global prominence last year after releasing surprisingly capable, low-cost open-source models that rattled assumptions about how much computing power and capital was truly needed to compete at the frontier of AI development. This latest funding round suggests the company is now moving toward a very different phase, one that looks increasingly similar to the capital-intensive strategies being pursued by its larger American rivals, even as it continues to lean on open-source releases as a point of differentiation.

Turning to national news, Western Sydney's new international airport is set to open today, becoming Australia's first new international airport in roughly three decades and operating on a curfew-free, twenty four hour schedule designed to relieve pressure on the long-congested Kingsford Smith Airport in Sydney's east. Construction was completed on budget and ahead of schedule, though the airport opens with relatively few flights scheduled in its early weeks and no direct rail link until sometime next year, prompting some transport researchers to describe passenger forecasts as somewhat optimistic in the near term. Independent economists have suggested it could take up to a decade for the airport to become genuinely profitable, warning that a broader economic downturn, shifting attitudes toward immigration, or simply a slower than expected ramp-up in passenger numbers could all extend that timeline further. Regional leaders in Sydney's west are hoping the airport becomes the anchor for a much larger economic hub, attracting industry and investment to a part of the city that's historically struggled with distance from the CBD and comparatively limited local job opportunities. Local councils have also flagged that surrounding public transport and road infrastructure still needs to catch up with the scale of the aviation project, with some nearby residents currently facing longer trips to the brand new airport than they would to the existing one on the other side of the city.

In Queensland, the state government has significantly wound back fish passage requirements for dam, weir, and other waterway barrier construction, arguing the change cuts red tape and project costs without compromising protections for native fish populations. Under the new rules, more than ninety per cent of waterway barrier works, culverts, disaster recovery projects, and dam and weir repairs no longer require a separate development application specifically addressing fish passage, a change that also extends to roughly one hundred and twenty two thousand kilometres of waterways across western Queensland. The government says it's already assessed over a hundred referable dams and weirs under the new approach, finding that only around ten per cent of them actually required a fish passage to be built in, with construction costs for such structures ranging from a couple of million dollars for smaller weirs up to a reported quarter of a billion dollars for the largest dams. Officials stress that fish passages remain a condition of approval wherever they are genuinely required, and that other federal and state protections, including assessments for threatened species, still apply regardless of this specific change. The government is framing the move as a practical efficiency measure for water infrastructure projects, though independent assessment of the change's actual impact on fish populations and river ecosystems over time has not yet been widely published.

And on the Gold Coast, Chaz Mostert powered to victory in Saturday's opening Gold Coast 500 race, held in wet and increasingly treacherous conditions at the Surfers Paradise street circuit, a win that also secures his place in the Supercars championship's upcoming Sandown semi-finals. Mostert overtook pole-sitter Broc Feeney within the opening lap and went on to win by more than twenty seconds, while championship leader Matt Payne crossed the line second on the road but was handed a time penalty for exceeding track limits, ultimately dropping him to third behind veteran driver David Reynolds. The slippery conditions produced a string of incidents throughout the race, including a spin at the beach chicane, a costly error from one of the weekend's title contenders, and at least one retirement due to mechanical failure, adding plenty of drama for the thousands of fans who braved the rain to watch trackside. With a second championship race still to come and wet weather forecast to continue lingering over the region, drivers and teams are bracing for potentially even more unpredictable conditions as the Gold Coast 500 weekend heads toward its conclusion.
