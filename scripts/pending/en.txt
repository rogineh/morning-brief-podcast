Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Saturday, the 31st of October, 2026. Here's what's happening today in technology and AI, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, French AI company Mistral has released the full open weights for its latest flagship model, Mistral Large Four, nicknamed "Le Chonk," making a roughly one-trillion-parameter system freely available for anyone to download, inspect, and run on their own hardware. The model uses a mixture-of-experts design, meaning it only activates a fraction of its total parameters, somewhere around fifty billion, for any given task, which keeps it more efficient to run despite its enormous overall size. Mistral is positioning the model as the strongest openly available option for coding tasks, while also highlighting strong performance on cybersecurity and general reasoning benchmarks. The release follows a public preview that began earlier in October, with Mistral saying the open-weight version benefited from additional reinforcement learning training between preview and full release, delivering meaningful performance gains over the earlier version. The weights are available through Mistral's own platform and API, as well as through several third-party hosts, and a more compressed, quantised version has also been released for teams wanting to run the model on a smaller number of high-end graphics processors. The move continues a broader pattern this year of major AI labs outside the United States, particularly in Europe and China, pushing to establish openly available alternatives to the large proprietary models dominating the American market, often framing openness as a matter of technological sovereignty for governments and companies wary of depending entirely on a handful of overseas providers.

Turning to national news, fresh inflation figures showing prices rising four-point-three per cent over the year to September have sharply increased expectations that the Reserve Bank will lift interest rates again when its board meets this coming Monday. The underlying measure of inflation that the central bank watches most closely, which strips out volatile items, climbed to three-point-seven per cent over the September quarter, coming in hotter than both market forecasts and the Reserve Bank's own earlier projections. Several major banks have shifted their official forecasts in response, with some now expecting the cash rate to rise to four-point-eight-five per cent on Monday, which would mark the central bank's fifth rate increase so far this year, while at least one major bank still expects policymakers to pause after any Monday move rather than keep tightening into the new year. Financial markets have moved quickly to price in a rate rise as the much more likely outcome, a sharp turnaround from expectations just a month ago, when traders had largely expected the Reserve Bank to hold steady. A deputy governor at the central bank warned only last week that the inflation outlook had become materially worse than policymakers had anticipated, language that's been read by economists as a strong signal the board is leaning toward further tightening despite the pressure higher rates continue to place on mortgage holders and household budgets more broadly.

In Queensland, irrigators across the state's share of the Murray-Darling Basin are being urged to move quickly to install government-subsidised water meter technology before a December deadline, with authorities warning that funding to cover the cost could run out before every eligible property is fitted. Under the requirements, holders of relevant water access entitlements need a compliant telemetry device fitted to their meters by the first of December this year, a deadline that was already pushed back by a year to give irrigators more time to comply. For those who act before funding runs dry, the subsidy covers the full cost of supplying and installing the device along with data transmission and maintenance for the first year of operation, after which ongoing data fees and meter validation become the water user's own responsibility. A government-contracted provider has been engaged to deliver the subsidised devices across more than a dozen water management areas, while a separate state-owned water authority is coordinating installation on meters it owns directly in some irrigation schemes. Officials say the push toward better telemetry is aimed at improving the accuracy and transparency of water use measurement across the basin, a long-standing point of contention between irrigators, environmental groups, and downstream water users in neighbouring states.

And on the Gold Coast, this year's Schoolies celebrations will include a ticketed event for the first time in more than two decades, with Year Twelve graduates set to pay seventy-five dollars to enter a fenced beachside precinct on one night of the week-long festivities. The ticketed night falls on the second official evening of Schoolies Week, which runs across the back half of November, and applies specifically to a paid music festival staged inside the existing Schoolies Hub precinct on Surfers Paradise beach, which graduates have previously been able to enter free of charge with a wristband throughout the week. The private company that runs the official Schoolies program says the change has been approved by the state government as a trial, and insists free entertainment will remain available at the same venue on the other six nights of the celebration. The Gold Coast's mayor says he wasn't informed about the new charge ahead of time and has voiced his opposition to it, arguing that Schoolies already deliver a significant economic boost to Surfers Paradise, where accommodation prices climb steeply during the celebration period. Organisers estimate roughly twelve thousand graduates celebrated on the Gold Coast last year, a figure that's fallen substantially over recent years from a peak of close to twenty thousand attendees.
