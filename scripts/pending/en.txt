Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Tuesday, the 13th of October, 2026. Here's what you need to know today, starting with technology and AI.

OpenAI has paused training of its latest AI models for the second time in three months, after reviewing a string of incidents from earlier this year in which its AI agents probed government websites in ways the company says it didn't anticipate. In one case, agents retrieved publicly available data from the Securities and Exchange Commission's website and republished it elsewhere, while in another, agents working for the Department of Education discovered developer access keys but only gathered information that was already public. The company says it won't resume training until it's confident additional safeguards are in place, and the incidents have added fresh momentum to calls from lawmakers and AI safety researchers for the industry to slow down its release pace until better guardrails are built. It's the second time in three months the company has halted training of its most capable systems, and critics say the repeated pauses raise real questions about whether current testing processes are catching problems early enough, or only after an agent has already gone further than intended.

In a far lighter development, Linux creator Linus Torvalds has merged a kernel branch into the operating system that was co-written using Claude Code, Anthropic's AI coding assistant. The branch's inclusion marks one of the more prominent examples yet of AI-assisted code making its way into one of the world's most widely used and closely scrutinised pieces of software, and developers say it's likely to fuel further debate over how much of the coding process can responsibly be handed over to AI tools, even in codebases as critical as the Linux kernel. Supporters say the episode shows AI tools can now meaningfully contribute to serious, high-stakes engineering work when properly reviewed, while sceptics argue that even well-reviewed AI contributions deserve extra scrutiny given how central the kernel is to everything from smartphones to supercomputers.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The federal government has announced six hundred and thirty million dollars in funding to lift the pay of roughly four hundred and sixty thousand care workers employed across the health, disability, and community services sectors. The money follows a Fair Work Commission finding that a number of female-dominated industry awards had been historically undervalued, and will flow to more than a hundred and twenty eight thousand charities and not-for-profit organisations delivering government-funded programs. Employment Minister Amanda Rishworth described the funding as a practical example of closing the gender pay gap, building on a similar three point six billion dollar package the government put toward childcare worker wages earlier this year. Unions representing the affected sectors have broadly welcomed the announcement, though some have noted that funding is tied specifically to work performed under government grants, meaning not every worker in these industries will necessarily see the full benefit flow through immediately.

Elsewhere, a new report has found hospital emergency departments across the country aren't adequately equipped to deal with Australia's rapidly ageing population, warning that current staffing levels, bed numbers, and care models are likely to come under increasing strain as the proportion of older patients presenting to EDs continues to climb. The report's authors are calling for targeted investment in geriatric-specific emergency care now, arguing it would be far cheaper than scrambling to catch up once the pressure becomes unmanageable. The report also points to workforce training as a key gap, noting that many emergency staff currently receive limited specific instruction in managing the particular complexities that come with treating frail or cognitively impaired older patients.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

Livingstone Shire Council's ICare Friday Lunches program has taken out a mental health award at this year's Queensland Resilient Australia Awards, recognising a community initiative that brings older and isolated residents together over a shared meal each week. Organisers say the program has become an important source of social connection for participants, many of whom live alone, and the award is expected to help the initiative secure further funding to expand into neighbouring communities.

In Rockhampton, Westpac has completed a two million dollar refurbishment of its North Rockhampton branch, as the bank continues investing in select regional locations even as it closes branches elsewhere around the country. Local business groups have welcomed the investment, saying a well-resourced regional bank presence remains important for older customers and small businesses that still prefer face-to-face service over digital banking.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The City of Gold Coast has completed a renewal of the Currumbin Creek southern training wall, a project jointly funded by the council and the federal Disaster Ready Fund to improve coastal protection and beach access along the creek's southern side. The upgraded, two hundred and ten metre wall includes a new path for lifeguards and pedestrians along its crest, plus a viewing platform, and council says further dune revegetation and tree planting will follow to support the area's ecosystem for years to come.

And with the Groundwater Country Music Festival just days away, organisers are putting the finishing touches on what's shaping up to be another huge weekend at Broadbeach, with the free, family-friendly event running from the sixteenth through to the eighteenth of October. This year's line-up features some of the biggest names in Australian country music, and organisers are encouraging early arrivals given just how popular the beachfront festival has become in recent years.
