First, technology and AI. US President Donald Trump has announced plans to create a new "AI Force" modelled on the Space Force, along with the appointment of an "AI czar" to oversee the industry's development, though he offered few details on what the new body would actually do, what budget it might have, or where exactly it would sit within the federal government. The announcement comes as Trump doubles down on his push to accelerate American AI development with limited regulation, a stance that puts him at odds with researchers who've warned the technology could spiral beyond human control without stronger safeguards, and it lands just days after Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei publicly called for the industry to slow down its most powerful systems. Meanwhile, in the AI industry itself, Alibaba's Qwen team has released a new omnimodal model capable of jointly processing text, images, audio, and video with a million-token context window, claiming a substantial performance jump over its predecessor, part of a broader wave of rapid-fire model releases out of Chinese AI labs this month as competition in the open-weight space continues to intensify. Separately, a small Beijing-based startup founded earlier this year by a Tsinghua University professor has reportedly reached a valuation of well over a billion dollars after a series of funding rounds from major Chinese technology investors, with the company taking an unusual approach of refining existing open-weight models rather than training new ones from scratch, a strategy its backers say lets a lean team of under a hundred people compete with far larger, better-resourced labs.

Now to national news here in Australia. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has formally launched Australia's campaign for a two-year seat on the United Nations Security Council, running under the slogan "We listen, we partner, we protect," with cyber safety and online security positioned at the heart of the pitch. The campaign argues that cyberattacks, artificial intelligence, and other digital threats now belong squarely in the realm of international peace and security, and comes as Australia and Finland currently stand uncontested for two seats reserved for the Western Europe and Others Group, though securing the position will still require a two-thirds majority of the UN General Assembly when members are elected in 2028. The push builds on Australia's recent world-first restrictions on social media for under-sixteens, which officials have increasingly used as a diplomatic calling card in talks with other governments considering similar rules. Government sources say the campaign will run over the next two years, with a formal diplomatic push planned across the Pacific, Southeast Asia, and Europe to build support ahead of the eventual vote, and officials framing Australia's pitch as a bridge-building one rather than a bloc-aligned candidacy, in contrast to some of the more contested campaigns from other regions in recent years.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. Police have charged eighty-one people with two hundred and sixty-five offences in the first month of Taskforce Yankee Tread, a dedicated crackdown on dangerous driving, illegal street racing, and burnout activity across the Moreton district. Officers have seized seven vehicles along with false, stolen, and altered number plates, immobilised twelve vehicles for ninety days over repeat offending, impounded one vehicle outright, and issued a further eighteen traffic infringement notices to eleven other people since the operation launched in mid-August, with police saying the results reflect a deliberate, sustained response to a spike in organised hooning events and street racing that had been causing growing concern among residents. Detectives have vowed the crackdown will continue in the weeks ahead, with more high-visibility patrols planned around known hotspots, and police say the results from just the first month show how deeply entrenched the hooning problem had become in parts of the district before the dedicated taskforce was stood up in mid-August. Officers have also pointed to a noticeable drop in reported incidents at some of the district's worst-affected intersections since the crackdown began, though they caution it's too early to say whether that trend will hold as the taskforce's presence becomes more widely known among local drivers.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. The city council has unanimously approved a one-and-a-half-million-dollar crackdown on illegal camping, deploying a dedicated ten-person compliance team, six officers by day and four overnight, to patrol parks and public spaces after the city received around seven thousand complaints about rough sleeping and unauthorised camping. The program is aimed specifically at antisocial and repeat offenders rather than vulnerable people doing it tough, with officers directed to refer anyone in genuine need to homelessness support services rather than simply moving them on, and council says the extra resourcing will also allow for stepped-up patrols around major events on the Gold Coast calendar. The move follows months of debate over how the city balances compassionate outreach with complaints from residents and businesses about impacts on public spaces, and councillors say they'll review the program's effectiveness after its first few months in operation. The vote was unanimous among councillors, a notable point of agreement after previous debates on the issue had exposed sharp differences over how heavy-handed enforcement should be, and homelessness support services say they're hopeful the referral pathway built into the new program will mean more people actually connect with help rather than simply being moved from one part of the city to another.
