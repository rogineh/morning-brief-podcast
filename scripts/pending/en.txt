In technology, growing stress in credit markets is raising fresh questions about how the artificial intelligence boom is actually being financed, with the cost of insuring against default on debt issued by major AI infrastructure players climbing to record levels in recent weeks. Specialist cloud computing provider CoreWeave has become the clearest flashpoint, with the market price of insuring its debt implying investors now see a significant chance of default within five years, and some of its bonds trading at distressed levels with yields well above what's typically considered investment grade. Even software giant Oracle, generally viewed as a far safer bet, has seen the cost of insuring its debt more than double since the start of the year, with one major ratings agency reportedly downgrading the company to just one notch above speculative grade. Social media giant Meta and chip maker Nvidia have both also seen the market's perceived risk of their own debt reach record highs recently, though both remain considerably less risky by this measure than smaller, more specialised AI infrastructure companies. Analysts say the common thread is that Oracle, networking and satellite company SpaceX, and chip designer Broadcom have together issued close to five hundred billion dollars in fresh debt this year specifically to help fund the enormous data centre build-out AI companies say they need, and bond markets are increasingly starting to ask harder questions about exactly how, and how reliably, all of that borrowed money eventually gets paid back.

Turning to national news, Australian home values have now fallen for seven consecutive months, with property analysts describing the pace of the current decline as the fastest in roughly forty years, coming just a day ahead of the Reserve Bank's next interest rate decision. Brisbane recorded the steepest monthly fall of any capital city in October, down two per cent, with values across the Queensland capital now down more than six per cent over just the past three months alone, a faster rate of decline than either Sydney or Perth over the same period. Industry researchers say persistently high interest rates remain the single biggest drag on buyer demand, compounded by broader affordability pressures and a noticeable pullback in investor activity following recent changes to tax settings affecting property investment. One major bank has forecast that prices nationally could ultimately fall by as much as fifteen per cent from their peak before the market finds a floor, though analysts note that many existing homeowners still retain substantial equity gains built up during the sharp price growth of recent years. Virtually every suburb across Brisbane, Perth, and Adelaide recorded some degree of price decline over the past three months, suggesting the downturn has become genuinely broad-based rather than confined to a handful of overheated pockets.

In Queensland, new figures show the state's visitor economy reached a record forty-five-point-six billion dollars in the year to June, outperforming official projections by close to one-point-four billion dollars and setting fresh benchmarks across both the international and domestic tourism markets. International visitor numbers climbed to around two-point-four million for the year, with those travellers collectively spending more than eight billion dollars in the state, representing a substantial jump in spending even as the overall increase in visitor numbers was more modest by comparison. Domestic tourism also performed strongly, with Australian travellers from other states spending close to thirty-seven-point-five billion dollars in Queensland over the same period, continuing several years of steady growth in locals choosing to holiday within the state rather than travelling overseas. Brisbane recorded particularly strong growth in international visitor spending, posting a jump of more than forty per cent to reach north of four billion dollars for the year, while the Whitsundays region also posted strong growth in its own visitor spending figures. Tourism officials say the results reflect sustained investment in attracting both international and domestic travellers to the state, and point to continued growth in coming years as further evidence that Queensland's broader tourism strategy is delivering tangible economic returns.

And on the Gold Coast, a freedom of information dispute has reignited scrutiny of Mayor Tom Tate's widely reported stay at Donald Trump's Mar-a-Lago estate, after council officials confirmed the mayor personally removed an invoice document from council offices that had previously helped clear him of a conflict of interest over a proposed Trump-branded tower development. Council records relating to the mayor's three-day stay reportedly contain only a brief, four-word entry describing it simply as a meeting with the Trump Organisation, with the mayor's office having since updated his personal register of interests to state that he paid for his own accommodation and transfers out of his own pocket. The mayor has previously said that once he received the relevant invoice, he promptly paid the amount himself and immediately updated his official declarations accordingly, though he has so far declined to produce that invoice even in response to a formal right-to-information request. Separately, the White House has previously disputed elements of the mayor's account of the visit, stating that Donald and Melania Trump did not personally dine with the mayor's travelling group as had been suggested at the time. The proposed Trump Tower development that originally prompted scrutiny of the trip was ultimately discontinued within a few months of the visit, though questions over the transparency of the mayor's declarations and the missing documentation have continued to linger regardless.
