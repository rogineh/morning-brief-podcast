In technology, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI have thrown their support behind a new shared standard that would let website publishers opt out of having their content used to train artificial intelligence models, using a single instruction added to the familiar robots dot txt file that already governs how search engines crawl the web. The mechanism, developed through an international internet standards body, introduces a specific policy token that publishers can add alongside their existing crawler rules, rather than requiring them to maintain a separate set of instructions for every individual AI company scraping their site. Google has indicated it will map the new opt-out signal to its existing Google-Extended designation, meaning publishers who opt out will also stop their content from being used to ground responses within the company's Gemini applications, not just traditional model training. Microsoft's Bing has signalled similar support, though with a notable caveat of its own, clarifying that the preference will govern how content is used rather than whether it can be crawled in the first place. The move represents a rare moment of coordination between major AI developers that are otherwise fiercely competitive, reflecting growing pressure from publishers and content creators who have struggled for years to keep pace with a rapidly shifting landscape of different, often conflicting, opt-out mechanisms across the industry.

Turning to national news, the federal government says a record number of doctors are now commencing general practice training this year, with roughly two thousand one hundred trainees beginning their GP studies in 2026, representing a fourteen per cent increase on the previous record set just last year. The announcement forms part of a broader effort to address long-standing concerns about general practitioner shortages, particularly in regional and outer suburban areas where access to a local doctor has become an increasingly difficult proposition for many families. Additional training places have also been flagged for 2027, with the government indicating more than two thousand new positions are already being planned for next year's intake as part of a sustained push to keep growing the pipeline of future GPs. Despite the positive headline figures, rural health advocates have continued pushing back, arguing the geographic distribution of these additional places still doesn't adequately address persistent workforce shortages in the areas that need general practitioners most urgently. Health officials maintain that building overall training capacity remains an essential first step regardless of distribution challenges, suggesting that addressing where graduates ultimately choose to practice will require a separate, ongoing policy effort beyond simply expanding trainee numbers.

In Queensland, the state government has confirmed a fifty-two million dollar investment in essential infrastructure works around Bundaberg, aimed at unlocking new residential land capable of eventually supporting more than four thousand seven hundred additional homes in the region. The funding will go toward trunk infrastructure such as roads, water, and sewerage works needed to service new housing developments, addressing a common bottleneck that has delayed otherwise shovel-ready residential projects in regional areas across the state. Officials say the investment forms part of a broader state-wide push to accelerate housing supply outside the heavily populated south-east corner, recognising that regional centres like Bundaberg have faced their own distinct affordability and availability pressures in recent years. Local council representatives have welcomed the funding, noting that without this kind of upfront infrastructure spending, many approved residential sites simply remain undeveloped regardless of underlying demand from prospective home buyers. The announcement adds to a series of similar infrastructure funding packages the state has rolled out across various regional centres this year, reflecting a consistent policy focus on removing practical barriers to new housing supply well beyond Brisbane and the Gold Coast.

And on the Gold Coast, City Council remains locked in an ongoing legal battle over so-called height uplift provisions in its planning scheme, after the Queensland Court of Appeal ruled against the council earlier this year in a case that's since placed billions of dollars in development approvals under a cloud of uncertainty. The dispute originated with a challenge to the council's approval of additional building height for a beachfront tower at Bilinga, with the Court of Appeal ultimately finding the planning court had misread a key provision relating to housing choice and affordability when the original approval was granted. Industry estimates suggest as much as five and a half billion dollars worth of approved or pending development across the city could potentially be affected depending on how the matter is ultimately resolved. Council has since applied to the High Court for special leave to appeal the ruling, a request that remains undetermined, while simultaneously exploring other options including possible changes to its planning scheme or seeking direct intervention from the state government to resolve the uncertainty sooner. Property industry figures say the prolonged legal uncertainty is already complicating financing and planning decisions for a number of major projects across the city, adding that a swift resolution, whichever way it ultimately falls, would be preferable to the current limbo for everyone involved.
