In technology, one of the major banks has issued a fresh warning to businesses about the growing sophistication of artificial intelligence-enabled scams, including business email compromise and impersonation fraud that increasingly uses AI tools to make fraudulent communications far more convincing than in the past. The bank says it prevented and recovered more than one hundred million dollars in scam and fraud-related funds over the past financial year, and that customer losses from scams fell by around a quarter compared with the previous year, a result it attributes partly to better detection systems and partly to growing public awareness. Even so, the bank warns that criminals are rapidly adapting, using AI to generate convincing fake voices, written communications, and even video in some cases, making it harder than ever for employees to rely on gut instinct alone when something feels slightly off. Business email compromise, where scammers impersonate a senior executive or trusted supplier to trick staff into redirecting payments, remains one of the most costly scam categories facing Australian businesses, and the bank is urging companies to put stronger verification processes in place, particularly for any request to change payment details or make an urgent transfer. Cybersecurity experts say the arms race between scammers using AI to craft more convincing attacks and banks using AI to detect them is likely to keep intensifying for the foreseeable future, with neither side able to claim a decisive advantage for long.

Turning to national news, the federal government's ban on foreign buyers purchasing existing homes remains a live point of debate in the broader conversation about housing affordability, even as officials acknowledge the measure affects a relatively small slice of the overall market. Analysts estimate the ban applies to less than half of one per cent of existing home sales nationally, with foreign investment in housing concentrated far more heavily in new-build developments, which remain largely unaffected by the restriction. Supporters of the policy argue that even a modest, symbolic measure helps address public frustration over housing affordability and sends a clear signal that the government is willing to act, while critics on both sides of politics say more substantial reforms are needed to meaningfully shift the balance between housing supply and demand. The debate comes amid continued pressure on rental markets in several capital cities, with vacancy rates remaining extremely tight in some areas and rents continuing to climb even as wage growth struggles to keep pace. Housing economists say addressing the underlying supply shortfall, through faster approvals, more skilled construction workers, and genuine coordination between state and federal governments, remains the far bigger lever available to policymakers than targeted restrictions on foreign buyers alone.

In Queensland, preparations continue for a major upgrade to the Queensland Tennis Centre ahead of the twenty thirty-two Olympic and Paralympic Games, with site investigation works now underway on land recently handed over to the authority coordinating Games infrastructure. The hundred-and-thirteen-million-dollar project will add a new three-thousand-seat show court arena alongside a dozen additional match courts, building on the venue's existing courts to create what organisers say will be a genuine centrepiece for Olympic and Paralympic tennis in Brisbane's south. The expansion follows through on funding commitments made as part of the state's broader review of Games venues, which concluded that upgrading the existing tennis centre made more sense than building an entirely new facility elsewhere. Tennis administrators say securing the Games has already helped drive renewed investment and interest in the sport across Queensland, from grassroots participation through to elite pathways, and that a genuinely world-class venue will help cement that legacy well beyond the Games themselves. Construction on the expanded courts and arena is expected to ramp up over the coming years as the broader Olympic infrastructure program accelerates across the state.

And on the Gold Coast, the Titans' proposed new training hub at Carrara has received a twenty-five-million-dollar commitment from the state government, a significant boost for the rugby league club's long-held ambition to build a dedicated, purpose-built facility to house its playing and administrative operations. The club has outgrown its existing training arrangements as its playing roster and support staff have expanded in recent years, and officials say a modern, centralised facility will help the Titans better compete with rival clubs that already enjoy purpose-built training bases of their own. The Carrara precinct, already home to major sporting and entertainment infrastructure, is seen as a natural fit for the project given its existing transport links and proximity to other sporting facilities in the area. Local sporting bodies have welcomed the funding commitment, saying a stronger, better-resourced Titans organisation delivers flow-on benefits for the broader Gold Coast sporting community, including junior development programs that rely on strong links to the professional club. Club officials say detailed design work will now progress, with a timeline for construction expected to be confirmed in the coming months.
