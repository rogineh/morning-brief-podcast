Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Tuesday, the 10th of November, 2026. Here's what's happening today in technology and AI, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, Airbnb has disclosed a related-party deal with a previously secretive artificial intelligence research lab founded and controlled by its own chief executive, Brian Chesky, in a quarterly filing lodged alongside its third-quarter results last week. According to that filing, the lab acquired certain intellectual property from Airbnb and reimbursed the company for some expenses, in exchange for cash consideration of around seven million dollars. In return, Airbnb secured the right to invest in future funding rounds for the lab at its own election, along with a license to use certain of the lab's technology internally. The filing confirms that Chesky holds a controlling equity interest in the lab and personally performs work for it, while Airbnb's own chief technology officer also holds an equity stake and contributes his time, prompting the company's board to route the arrangement through a special committee of independent directors under its related-party transaction policy. The lab itself was first reported on earlier this year and publicly confirmed by Chesky only a few months ago, with reporting suggesting he has largely self-funded its operations to date. The filing does not disclose the lab's formal name, its current valuation, or precisely what intellectual property changed hands, leaving plenty of unanswered questions about how closely Airbnb's own technology roadmap might end up intertwined with its chief executive's separate AI ambitions.

Turning to national news, Australian borrowers are continuing to absorb the fallout from last week's decision by the Reserve Bank to lift the official cash rate by a quarter of a percentage point to four point eight five per cent, its fifth increase so far this year and the highest level the cash rate has reached since 2008. The board's vote was notably split, with six members backing the increase and three preferring to hold rates steady, a divide the governor said shouldn't be read as a signal that the current tightening cycle is necessarily over. Major lenders have been moving this week to pass the increase on to variable mortgage holders in full, with banks required under existing rules to give borrowers at least twenty days' written notice before any higher repayment amount is actually debited from their account. Analysts estimate the cumulative effect of this year's five rate rises could add somewhere in the vicinity of six hundred dollars a month to repayments on an average-sized home loan, layering fresh pressure onto households already managing a difficult cost-of-living environment. All four major banks are said to be privately tipping this as the final hike of the current cycle, pointing to signs that underlying inflation pressures may finally be starting to ease, though the central bank itself has been notably reluctant to commit to that view publicly.

In Queensland, the state government has unveiled its preferred design for a replacement Bribie Island Bridge, settling on a four-lane structure that would finally relieve one of the south-east's most persistent transport bottlenecks after years of community campaigning for an upgrade. Under the plan, the existing bridge connecting the island to the mainland would be retained but repurposed as a dedicated shared pathway for pedestrians and cyclists, while all vehicle traffic shifts onto the new structure running alongside it. The project carries an estimated price tag in the vicinity of seven hundred and fifty million dollars, and officials say geotechnical investigation works are due to get underway in the coming days as planning moves into its next phase. Public feedback on the preferred design remains open until the twenty-seventh of November, giving residents and other interested parties a few more weeks to have their say before the design is locked in further. The state government has acknowledged it will also need cooperation from Canberra on environmental approvals before any construction timeline can be firmed up, meaning islanders hoping for relief from the bridge's long-standing congestion will likely need to remain patient for some time yet.

And on the Gold Coast, City Council has given the green light to another substantial addition to the Surfers Paradise skyline, approving a thirty-storey mixed-use tower known as Surfer on a consolidated site on Vista Street. The roughly two-hundred-million-dollar project from Gold Coast-based developer Bastion Group will combine twenty-five full residential apartments with seventy-five short-stay suites, alongside ground-floor retail space and a restaurant designed to activate the street frontage. The approval forms part of a broader pipeline of high-rise development the company has underway across the city, including its previously approved Nera tower currently under construction on Chevron Island. Industry figures say the project reflects continuing strong confidence in the Gold Coast's property and tourism markets, even as higher borrowing costs weigh on development finance more broadly across the sector following the latest run of interest rate increases. Local planning watchers note that Surfers Paradise in particular continues to attract a steady stream of new tower proposals, with several other projects in various stages of council assessment expected to be decided in the months ahead.
