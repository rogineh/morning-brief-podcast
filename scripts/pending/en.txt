In technology, energy company Drax has announced a major new partnership with investment giant Brookfield and AI infrastructure specialist Radiant to build a vast new artificial intelligence data centre at its power station site in North Yorkshire, England. The partners say the project could ultimately involve more than a gigawatt of computing capacity across three separate construction phases, with Brookfield estimating the total investment, including the specialised computer chips needed to fill the facility, could eventually exceed twenty billion pounds if the project is built out in full. Under the arrangement, Drax is contributing land, existing grid connections, and access to power, while Radiant will lead the design, customer engagement, and commercial side of the project, with capital flowing through a joint venture Brookfield established roughly a year ago specifically to fund AI infrastructure development in various markets around the world. The first phase is targeting commercial customers from early 2028, though that timeline depends on securing planning approval, regulatory sign-off, and at least one confirmed anchor tenant willing to commit before construction can begin in earnest. Later phases of the project could eventually see the data centre draw power directly from Drax's existing electricity generation under a long-term supply agreement, subject to further approvals, in a structure that illustrates how major energy companies are increasingly positioning themselves as direct infrastructure partners for the AI industry's enormous power needs.

Turning to national news, Australia's corporate regulator has issued a sharp public warning to superannuation funds over how they handle member complaints, after a review found roughly a quarter of the industry's trustees weren't properly analysing complaint data to spot wider systemic problems. The regulator's review covered all forty-five superannuation trustees operating in the country, and found a genuine mismatch between what funds themselves were detecting internally and what the financial ombudsman service was separately identifying, with some funds reporting virtually no systemic issues at all despite external complaint data suggesting otherwise. Complaint volumes handled by the financial ombudsman jumped more than forty per cent over the past financial year, reaching close to nine thousand complaints, a trend the regulator says should have been triggering far more internal scrutiny from fund trustees than it apparently was. The regulator's commissioner specifically criticised the industry's track record, saying superannuation is simply too important an asset for ordinary Australians to allow this scale of service failure and complaint-handling weakness to continue unaddressed. Separately, the review also flagged widespread and concerning delays across the industry in processing death benefit claims, with the regulator calling on funds to substantially overhaul how they manage this particularly sensitive category of claims going forward, and warning that it's prepared to consider formal enforcement action against funds that fail to lift their standards.

In Queensland, clean-up efforts are continuing across the state's south-east today after severe supercell thunderstorms swept through yesterday, dumping giant hailstones and leaving thousands of homes without power across affected regions. The worst of the hail fell overnight on the Fraser Coast, with stones reportedly reaching up to ten centimetres in diameter, before fresh supercells developed over inland parts of southern Queensland and tracked across Brisbane, Ipswich, and the Scenic Rim through the afternoon and evening. Energy network operators reported thousands of homes losing power as the storms brought down trees and power lines across multiple local government areas, with crews working through yesterday and continuing into today to restore supply to the hardest-hit pockets of the network. A Bureau of Meteorology forecaster had warned ahead of yesterday's system that conditions remained highly unstable, with the potential for destructive wind gusts, giant hail, and even isolated tornado activity in the most intense storm cells. Authorities are continuing to urge residents in affected areas to steer well clear of any fallen power lines, treating all downed wires as live regardless of appearances, while clean-up crews work to clear debris and restore essential services across the affected region.

And on the Gold Coast, the city council's decision to hand over day-to-day management of all eight of its public aquatic centres to a single private operator is set to formally take effect in the coming days, following a vote by councillors earlier this year. Under the new arrangement, the council will retain ownership of every aquatic centre and will continue setting entry fees and charges across the network, while the appointed private operator takes over the practical running of the facilities, including staffing and day-to-day operations. The chosen operator already manages two of the city's aquatic centres under existing arrangements, and will now extend its reach across the remaining council-run sites as part of the broader consolidation under a single strategic operating partner. The decision wasn't without controversy, with some councillors and community members raising concerns during earlier debate about the potential impact on staff pay and conditions, as well as questions about service quality and ongoing community access once a single operator is responsible for the entire network. Council has sought to reassure residents that all eight centres will remain open and city-owned throughout the transition, with customers able to continue using their local pool and fitness facilities as normal while the handover to the new operator takes place behind the scenes.
