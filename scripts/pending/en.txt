Turning now to news from across Australia.

The country's unusually warm and dry spring looks set for a significant break this week, with meteorologists forecasting a substantial rain system to move across the eastern two-thirds of the country over the coming days. Queensland is expected to see falls of between twenty five and one hundred and fifty millimetres in some areas, with northern New South Wales also forecast for a solid soaking, following what climate records show was the second warmest September on record nationally and the second driest September on record for the Murray-Darling Basin specifically. Agricultural analysts say the rain will be welcomed by many farmers, particularly those in areas where dry conditions have already pushed fire danger ratings higher than usual for this point in the season, though they caution that a single rain event, however substantial, won't fully reverse months of below-average soil moisture in the driest affected regions. Bushfire authorities say they'll be watching closely to see how much the rain actually eases the elevated fire risk that's already prompted a run of blazes across parts of Queensland and New South Wales in recent weeks. Water authorities in some inland catchments say even a modest inflow from this system would be welcome after a dry winter and spring, though they caution that sustained follow-up rainfall over coming months will matter far more to longer term water security than any single short-term weather event.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

Cairns Regional Council says a sustained advocacy push has delivered hundreds of millions of dollars in state and federal funding commitments for the region over the past year, covering housing, water security, and major infrastructure projects. Council representatives say the funding reflects years of persistent lobbying aimed at ensuring far north Queensland receives a fair share of government investment relative to the state's more populous south east corner, where the bulk of infrastructure spending has traditionally been concentrated. Local business groups have welcomed the funding wins, saying sustained investment in housing and water security in particular is essential groundwork for supporting the region's continued population and tourism growth over the coming decade. The council says it will continue pushing for further commitments in areas like transport and health infrastructure, arguing that far north Queensland's growing population increasingly justifies a larger slice of the state's overall infrastructure budget. Regional development experts say sustained, multi-year funding commitments of this kind matter more for a region's long-term trajectory than one-off grants, since they give councils and private investors enough certainty to plan larger projects years in advance rather than reacting to funding announcements as they're made.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

Plans are progressing for the Gold Coast's first dedicated surf park, with construction on the three hundred million dollar Palm Valley development at Parkwood expected to begin later this year following state government backing through its Tourism Icon Investment Fund. The project centres on a four hectare dual zone surfing lagoon using wave generating technology capable of producing consistent, surfable waves independent of ocean conditions, alongside a high performance surf training academy and a ten million dollar redevelopment of the site's existing golf course. Later stages of the project are expected to add villas, hotel accommodation, and additional commercial buildings, though those elements remain further out on the development timeline than the surf park itself. Tourism operators say a dedicated surf park would give the Gold Coast a genuinely novel drawcard independent of ocean and weather conditions, complementing rather than competing with the city's existing beach-based tourism appeal. Surf industry figures say wave pool technology has matured significantly in recent years, to the point where consistent, high quality artificial waves are now considered a genuine training and competition tool for serious surfers, rather than simply a novelty attraction for casual visitors.
