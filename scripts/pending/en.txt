In technology, the maintainers of the PHP programming language have rushed out emergency patches for two critical security flaws after they were uncovered using frontier AI models rather than traditional manual testing. The vulnerabilities, found independently by an AI safety research group working with OpenAI's models and separately by Google-owned security firm Wiz, allow unauthenticated attackers to run their own code on a vulnerable server before any application code even executes. Researchers say every version of PHP released over roughly the past decade is affected by at least one of the two flaws, and because PHP still powers a very large share of the world's websites, the scope of exposure is enormous. Fixed versions have already been released, and the practical advice from security researchers is straightforward: patch as soon as possible, since some reports suggest exploit attempts may already be circulating following an earlier-than-planned leak of technical details. OpenAI says this is one of several significant vulnerabilities its models have now helped uncover, and that it expects AI-assisted vulnerability discovery, for better and for worse, to become dramatically more common over the coming months, raising fresh questions about how quickly defenders can patch systems compared with how quickly attackers might weaponise the same discoveries. Security teams at large organisations around the world spent much of yesterday scrambling to confirm whether their own systems were exposed, a reminder of just how much critical infrastructure still quietly depends on decades-old code that few people think about until something like this forces the issue.

Turning to national news, several Australian government websites were temporarily taken offline yesterday as agencies rushed to apply the emergency PHP patches, though the Industry Minister moved quickly to reassure the public that no government systems appear to have been breached as a result of the vulnerability. The brief outages affected a number of federal agency websites that rely on the affected software, with technical teams working through the day to apply fixes and bring services back online once patching was confirmed complete. The parliamentary committee responsible for overseeing AI policy has asked for a formal briefing on exactly what happened, reflecting growing political interest in how government agencies monitor and respond to vulnerabilities, particularly ones discovered with the help of AI tools rather than human researchers alone. The episode comes at a time when concern about the security of ageing but widely used internet infrastructure has been rising, with officials noting that the speed of the response, rather than the existence of the vulnerability itself, may end up being the more important story in the long run. Opposition cyber security spokespeople have called for a broader review of how many government systems still rely on ageing software stacks, arguing yesterday's scramble shouldn't be treated as a one-off event but as a warning about the underlying state of public sector technology more generally.

In Queensland, beekeepers around Townsville are on alert after testing confirmed that Varroa mites detected at several sites in the region belong to a chemical-resistant strain, adding North Queensland to a growing list of areas where the pest has developed resistance to standard treatments. The Townsville Beekeepers Association is urging members to test their hives regularly and says affected beekeepers may now need to turn to mechanical removal methods, heat treatments, or alternative chemical groups, since resistant mites tend to cause more severe colony damage than the standard strain. Resistance was first detected in northern New South Wales earlier this year before spreading into parts of south-east Queensland, and authorities say rotating between different treatment types remains the best defence against resistance developing or spreading further. The pest poses a serious threat to both commercial beekeeping operations and the broader agricultural sector, given how heavily crop pollination across the state depends on healthy bee populations. Industry groups say they're watching the situation closely, given how costly and disruptive previous Varroa outbreaks have proven for the honey and pollination industries in other states, and are encouraging even hobbyist beekeepers with just a hive or two in the backyard to take part in regular testing, since untreated backyard hives can become a source of reinfection for commercial operations nearby.

And on the Gold Coast, the city council's full meeting was due to deliver its final verdict yesterday on a long-running proposal to build a twenty three storey apartment tower on the site of Coolangatta's historic Jazzland Dance Hall, with the council's planning committee having already recommended refusing the application. The developer, BeckDev, has pursued various versions of the project for several years, at one point reducing the proposed height to eighteen storeys after more than four hundred and seventy community objections, before later returning with taller designs including the current twenty three storey proposal. An earlier, shorter version of the tower was previously rejected by the council, a decision that was then upheld by the Planning and Environment Court after a legal challenge. Local heritage and community groups have consistently opposed building at that height on the site, arguing it would overshadow the surrounding low-rise character of the area, while the developer has maintained that higher density is appropriate for a block so close to the Coolangatta beachfront. Whatever the council's final call, the long-running dispute over the site looks likely to remain a reference point in future debates about building heights along the Gold Coast's southern beaches.
