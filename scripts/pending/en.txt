In technology, Abu Dhabi has signed four separate agreements with OpenAI covering government services, energy, investment, and education, as the emirate pushes to become what it describes as the world's first fully AI-native government. Under the centrepiece government deal, officials will roll out what's being called a machine-readable government initiative, using AI-powered systems to help assess routine regulatory compliance matters such as permit and certificate applications, though officials have stressed that human staff will retain final decision-making authority rather than handing that responsibility entirely to automated systems. State energy giant ADNOC has separately agreed to become the first energy company in the world to deploy OpenAI's frontier technology within its own operations, while sovereign wealth fund Mubadala has signed its own agreement to apply advanced AI capabilities to investment analysis, opportunity identification, and risk assessment work. A fourth, broader five-year partnership aims to support AI adoption more generally across government agencies, private enterprises, and educational institutions throughout the UAE, building on an earlier training initiative designed to equip twenty thousand government employees and members of the wider public with practical AI skills. The agreements were signed following a meeting between Abu Dhabi's Crown Prince and OpenAI's chief executive, and reflect a broader pattern of Gulf states moving aggressively to position themselves as early, large-scale adopters of frontier AI technology across both government and major state-owned enterprises.

Turning to national news, the Reserve Bank's board meets today to decide on interest rates, with financial markets overwhelmingly expecting the central bank to deliver its fifth rate increase of the year, lifting the cash rate from four-point-six per cent to four-point-eight-five per cent. Money markets are currently pricing in roughly an eighty per cent chance of that quarter-point increase going ahead, with only a relatively small probability currently assigned to the central bank instead holding rates steady for another month. The decision comes against the backdrop of inflation data released late last month that came in hotter than expected, alongside separate figures showing Australian home values have now posted their fastest rate of decline in roughly four decades, as years of cumulative rate rises continue weighing heavily on household budgets and borrowing capacity. Economists will be watching closely not just for today's decision itself, due early this afternoon, but also for the accompanying quarterly economic forecasts the central bank publishes alongside its rate announcements, which should offer fresh clues about how much further tightening policymakers think might still be needed heading into next year. Mortgage holders bracing for another rate rise have had little reprieve this year, with today's decision set to mark the fifth increase since the Reserve Bank began its current tightening cycle, a pace of rate rises that's drawn growing criticism from some economists who argue the cumulative impact on the broader economy is becoming harder to justify.

In Queensland, the state remains the only jurisdiction in the country that hasn't signed onto a national early childhood disability support program known as Thriving Kids, more than a month after the initiative's first phase officially began rolling out everywhere else. The program is designed to support children aged eight and under who have low-to-moderate support needs outside the main disability insurance scheme, with the broader national rollout expected to be fully complete by early 2028. Queensland's state government has pointed to concerns about cost-shifting from the Commonwealth and says it hasn't received adequate explanation of key details, including precisely how the program's threshold for low-to-moderate support needs would actually be defined and applied in practice. The federal government has pushed back firmly, arguing Queensland agreed to participate in this broader system of support outside the core disability scheme earlier this year in exchange for a substantial package of additional Commonwealth hospital funding. Disability advocacy groups have become increasingly vocal in urging Queensland to sign on, warning there's currently no clear national roadmap for families whose children fall outside the specific age range and support-level criteria the Thriving Kids program covers. With talks between Queensland and the Commonwealth still unresolved a full month into the national rollout, parents of eligible Queensland children remain uncertain about exactly what support, if any, will be available to them in the near term.

And on the Gold Coast, the city council has dropped its long-running legal fight against a controversial quarry expansion at Oxenford, abandoning its push for an outright refusal after originally rejecting the proposal back in 2021. The expansion would allow the Nucrush-operated quarry to dramatically increase its annual production from around three hundred and fifty thousand tonnes to as much as one million tonnes, while also extending the site's approved operating life by a significant number of years beyond its original end date. Council had previously engaged more than a dozen expert witnesses along with specialist planning lawyers and barristers in its efforts to block the expansion, following five hundred and seventy-six public submissions on the proposal, many from residents opposed to the project's scale and potential local impacts. Rather than continuing to fight for a full refusal, the council says its focus has now shifted toward securing the strictest possible operating conditions should the expansion ultimately proceed, with the final decision on the matter now resting with Queensland's Planning and Environment Court rather than the council itself. Local community groups that campaigned against the expansion have expressed disappointment at the council's shift in strategy, while the court case itself is expected to continue working its way through the legal process for some time yet before a final resolution is reached.
