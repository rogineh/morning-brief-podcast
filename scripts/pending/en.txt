Turning to national news, Australia's consumer watchdog is developing the country's first genuinely national safety standard specifically for electric bikes, after years of patchwork state-based rules failed to keep pace with a growing number of fires and safety incidents linked to poorly made batteries and electrical systems. The regulator was formally directed by the federal government earlier this year to develop nationally consistent standards under existing consumer law, with e-bikes treated as the first priority ahead of other electric micromobility devices like scooters and skateboards that are expected to follow later. Stakeholder consultation on the proposed standard is expected to begin in the coming weeks, with the regulator then preparing a formal recommendation to government on whether to proceed with a new mandatory safety standard covering the devices. Experts who've studied the issue argue any effective standard needs to properly define what actually counts as a genuine e-bike versus other more powerful motorised devices sometimes marketed using similar terminology, while also covering the complete electrical system of a device rather than simply focusing on battery cells in isolation. The push for stronger standards comes as some state and territory governments have already moved independently to seize and destroy illegally modified or overpowered e-bikes, reflecting growing concern that current rules have allowed genuinely dangerous motorised devices onto Australian streets under the more innocuous e-bike label.

In Queensland, elders from the state's Australian South Sea Islander community are calling for a genuine national conversation about reparations and historical justice, with the community's peak advocacy body formally requesting a meeting with the Prime Minister to discuss the issue directly. The council's president says the broader question of reparations has come up repeatedly in conversations with elders during his travels along the Queensland coast, reflecting a community desire to see the issue properly addressed at the highest level of government rather than left unresolved indefinitely. The Australian South Sea Islander community traces its origins to more than fifty thousand people, overwhelmingly men, who were brought to Australia from roughly eighty different Pacific islands between 1863 and 1904, many under coercive or outright deceptive conditions that amounted to a form of slavery. While the community has achieved some formal recognition in recent years, including specific cultural rights protections in Queensland, advocates argue meaningful reparations and a proper national reckoning with this history still haven't genuinely materialised. It's not yet clear whether a meeting between the council and the Prime Minister has actually been scheduled, or what position the federal government might ultimately take on the broader reparations question being raised.

And on the Gold Coast, a sobering new report has found that none of nearly fifteen hundred rental properties surveyed across the city were affordable for nurses, cleaners, hospitality workers, retail staff, or community services workers earning typical full-time wages in those essential roles. The annual rental affordability snapshot found just three properties across the entire survey were within reach for paramedics or firefighters, while only six were considered affordable for a full-time school teacher, highlighting just how severely the city's rental market has outpaced wage growth in many essential occupations. A separate, more targeted snapshot focused specifically on low-income households found zero properties available on the Gold Coast for people relying on income support payments, and just a single property affordable for someone earning the minimum wage. Housing advocates say the findings illustrate a genuinely urgent problem, warning that essential workers being effectively priced out of the communities they serve ultimately creates broader costs and risks for everyone relying on those services. Nationally, the same broader survey found cleaners could afford less than one per cent of surveyed rental listings, with nurses only slightly better placed, underscoring that the Gold Coast's severe affordability crunch reflects a much wider pattern playing out in rental markets right across the country.
