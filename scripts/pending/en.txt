In the world of artificial intelligence, Anthropic has filed for its initial public offering, and the prospectus is drawing attention for its candour. Rather than the usual upbeat sales pitch, the filing lays out blunt warnings for potential investors about the risks posed by its own technology, including scenarios in which advanced models could act to resist being shut down or otherwise pursue goals contrary to their operators' intentions. It's an unusual approach for a company preparing to go public, and it reflects a broader shift in how the leading AI labs are talking about the technology they're building, less as an unambiguous triumph and more as something that still carries real risk alongside its promise.

Meanwhile, OpenAI has pulled back on a release it had been expected to ship this month. The company had planned to launch a new model, internally referred to as GPT-6.1 Astra, but shelved the rollout after internal testing found it fell short of the safety and alignment standards the company had set for itself. OpenAI hasn't given a firm timeline for when, or in what form, the model might eventually appear, but the decision underscores how seriously the major labs say they're treating pre-release testing as public scrutiny of AI safety continues to intensify.

On the product side, Meta has unveiled a new AI-powered application built around a team of autonomous digital assistants capable of handling everyday tasks on a person's behalf, things like ordering groceries, booking travel, and keeping household finances organised. It's the clearest sign yet that the big technology companies see "agentic" AI, tools that don't just answer questions but actually go out and complete tasks, as the next major battleground for consumer attention.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The Reserve Bank has released a summary of submissions it received as part of its ongoing review into the regulation of Australia's payments system. The review has been examining how rules first designed for cheques and card terminals should evolve to keep pace with digital wallets, buy-now-pay-later services, and real-time transfers, and the submissions reflect a wide range of views from banks, fintech companies, and consumer groups about how much oversight new payment methods should face.

Elsewhere, communities in the Murray-Darling Basin are voicing fresh concerns as authorities consider further water buybacks. Irrigators and basin towns have long argued that reductions in agricultural water allocations, even when aimed at restoring river health, come at a real cost to regional economies, and the latest round of proposals has reignited that long-running tension between environmental and agricultural priorities in one of the country's most contested water systems.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

The state government has confirmed it will take over responsibility for assessing data centre development applications, stripping that power away from local councils. Under the new framework, Queensland will become the "responsible assessor and coordinator" for these projects statewide, a significant shift given the state currently has no dedicated planning pathway for data centres at all, unlike New South Wales and Victoria. The change comes as Australia's largest proposed data centre, a project worth an estimated thirty-two billion dollars, moves through assessment in the Western Downs region west of Brisbane, with some local residents expressing concern about being left out of a process that will now run through Brisbane rather than their own council chambers. The new system will reportedly mirror recent changes to renewable energy approvals, requiring a social impact assessment and a community benefit agreement with the local council before any application can even be lodged.

In law enforcement news, Queensland Police say they're seizing an average of six illegal e-mobility devices every single day, as officers continue a crackdown on non-compliant e-bikes and e-scooters across the state. Authorities say many of the seized devices have been modified well beyond legal speed and power limits, raising safety concerns for riders and pedestrians alike, and police are urging anyone buying an e-bike or scooter to check it meets the legal requirements before taking it on the road.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The BLEACH Festival is now into its second week, with the eleven-day celebration of contemporary arts continuing to bring installations, performances, and public art across the city through until the eleventh of October. Organisers say the festival's spring shift to October has given this year's program a different energy compared to previous summer editions, with cooler evenings drawing steady crowds to the foreshore events.

Looking ahead, Gold Coast Open House returns this coming weekend, the tenth and eleventh of October, giving residents a rare chance to get behind the scenes at some of the city's most significant infrastructure and buildings, including Hinze Dam, the Gold Coast Desalination Plant, and the Palm Beach Aquatic and Community Centre. The free program is designed to give the public a better understanding of how the city's water and community infrastructure actually works, and organisers are encouraging families to plan ahead given spots at some venues are expected to fill quickly.

And the council has also flagged the Mayor's annual Seniors Concert, set for the thirteenth of October at HOTA, Home of the Arts, promising a disco-themed celebration with live music and acrobatic performances for the city's older residents.
