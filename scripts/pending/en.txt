First, technology and AI. Chinese technology giant Alibaba has unveiled a sweeping new strategy spanning artificial intelligence models, custom chips, and data centre infrastructure, including plans to train a new model with somewhere between five and ten trillion parameters, a scale that would place it among the largest AI systems ever attempted if realised. Alongside the model announcement, the company revealed what it describes as China's most powerful AI chip to date, part of a broader push to reduce the country's reliance on foreign semiconductor technology amid ongoing export restrictions from the United States and its allies. Industry watchers say the announcement underscores how quickly the frontier of AI development is broadening beyond the small handful of American labs that dominated headlines for much of the past few years, with Chinese firms increasingly racing to match or exceed Western capabilities across both software and the underlying hardware needed to run it. The scale of Alibaba's ambitions also raises fresh questions about the enormous energy and computing infrastructure such a model would require, at a time when data centre construction bottlenecks have already become a recurring theme in the industry both in the US and elsewhere. Alibaba has not given a firm timeline for when the larger model might be trained or released, and analysts note that reaching the upper end of the announced parameter range would likely require years of sustained investment in chip production and power infrastructure, even for a company with Alibaba's resources.

Now to national news here in Australia. New analysis spanning twenty-four years of survey data has found the long-term decline in mental health among Australian adolescents and young adults appears to be stabilising, with the steepest drop recorded between 2019 and 2021 now showing early signs of a rebound. Researchers caution the recovery is fragile and uneven, with young women continuing to report substantially higher rates of psychological distress than young men, and are calling for sustained investment in youth mental health services to consolidate the gains rather than risk losing momentum. The researchers say the pandemic years were the clear turning point in the data, and that understanding exactly which support measures introduced since then have helped will be crucial to locking in further improvement rather than allowing rates to drift backward as social and economic pressures continue to evolve. Meanwhile, all eyes are on Melbourne today ahead of this afternoon's AFL grand final between Brisbane and Fremantle, with fans travelling from across the country for the occasion, including one couple who flew in from the remote Western Australian town of Karratha to get married outside the MCG before taking their seats to watch the Dockers chase a maiden premiership. The match caps off what's been billed as one of the more compelling grand finals in years, pitting a club chasing history against genuine underdogs desperate for a first flag since joining the league three decades ago.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. The Dolphins are preparing for tonight's NRL preliminary final against the Sydney Roosters, in what could be the final game of veteran forward Ray Stone's career regardless of the result, with the experienced campaigner telling teammates he wants to leave everything on the field in what may be his last outing in the NRL. A win would send the Dolphins through to just their second grand final since joining the competition, giving the young club and its fans a shot at silverware that seemed unthinkable only a few seasons ago. Separately, organisers behind Queensland's bid for a seventeenth NRL licence say the region's junior rugby league pathway, with more than six thousand registered young players, gives the proposal a genuine foundation to build on, pointing to the success story of the North Queensland Cowboys as evidence that a new club rooted in local talent can thrive. Backers of the bid say formal talks with the league are ongoing, though they acknowledge a decision on expansion is unlikely to be made quickly given the scale of investment and planning any new licence would require.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. Prosecutors have formally dropped a murder charge against former bikie club member Paul Younan over the 2009 shooting death of Omega Ruston at Burleigh Heads, with the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions saying there was no reasonable prospect of conviction based on the evidence currently available. Younan was the last of three men who'd faced a murder charge over Ruston's killing, though he still faces twenty-seven separate perjury charges relating to the long-running case, which are scheduled to be heard in the Brisbane Magistrates Court next month. The case has spanned more than a decade and multiple court proceedings, and its latest turn is likely to reignite scrutiny of how long-running cold case prosecutions are pursued when key evidence becomes difficult to substantiate years after the fact. Legal observers say the outcome will likely prompt renewed debate over how prosecutors weigh the cost and prospects of pursuing decade-old cases through to trial, particularly when witness memories and physical evidence have had many years to deteriorate.
