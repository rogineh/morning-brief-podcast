In technology, Australia's education ministers have agreed to a nationwide digital detox for schools, significantly limiting how generative AI tools can be used in classrooms as part of a broader overhaul of the national curriculum. Under the new approach, primary school students will be barred from using generative AI tools during class time altogether, while high school students will face specific restrictions preventing them from using these tools during formal assessments and exams. The changes follow growing concern among educators and child development experts about the impact of unrestricted classroom technology use on genuine learning outcomes, attention spans, and academic integrity more broadly. A dedicated task force will now examine how individual states and territories can continue regulating screen time in classrooms going forward, with that body expected to report back to ministers on three separate occasions throughout next year as the policy continues to evolve. Some state and territory governments reportedly still need further convincing that harsher restrictions beyond today's agreed changes are genuinely necessary, suggesting today's announcement may represent an initial step rather than a final settled position on how AI tools should be handled in Australian classrooms long-term. Education ministers separately used today's meeting to announce a review of the primary maths curriculum, along with new mandatory lessons covering democracy, financial literacy, and the history of Jewish contributions to Australian society, signalling a genuinely broad overhaul extending well beyond just how schools handle generative AI.

Turning to national news, an independent public inquiry into the AUKUS submarine partnership has delivered a scathing final report, describing the arrangement as the worst, riskiest, and most costly defence decision in Australian history, and calling for the entire nuclear submarine program to be scrapped. The unofficial inquiry, crowdfunded and led by a former federal minister alongside a retired navy admiral, recommends suspending the agreement entirely, cancelling planned rotational submarine basing arrangements in Western Australia, and instead pursuing a different naval procurement path going forward. The report also raises concerns about additional costs potentially reaching well beyond a hundred billion dollars specifically tied to managing nuclear waste generated under the AUKUS arrangement, a figure the inquiry says hasn't been properly disclosed through official government channels to date. The opposition's defence spokesman has dismissed the inquiry as a sham, accusing the academics and former officials behind it of approaching the review with a predetermined conclusion already in mind, and specifically criticising the report's characterisation of the broader strategic risk posed by China. Because the inquiry carries no official government standing, its recommendations don't carry direct legal or policy weight, though the strength of its criticism and the prominence of the figures involved are likely to keep pressure on the government to defend the multi-billion-dollar submarine program publicly in the weeks ahead.

In Queensland, human rights advocates have strongly criticised the state government after officials blocked a visiting United Nations special rapporteur from touring a youth detention centre in Townsville during his official visit to Australia this week. The planned inspection of the facility, along with a separate visit to a youth detention centre in the Northern Territory, was cancelled after both state and territory governments declined to properly engage with the rapporteur's official human rights mandate. A Queensland government spokesperson didn't directly explain the reasoning behind the refusal, saying only that the government wished the visiting UN official well for the remainder of his broader Australian visit. Amnesty International Australia described the blocked visits as part of a troubling and recurring pattern of Australian governments obstructing legitimate international human rights scrutiny of youth detention conditions. The Townsville facility in question had reportedly been operating above its safe capacity limit when it was last formally inspected, adding further weight to advocates' concerns about transparency and oversight at youth detention centres more broadly across the state.

And on the Gold Coast, the city has officially kicked off hosting the Pan Pacific Masters Games today, a major ten-day multi-sport event expected to draw more than sixteen thousand competitors and supporters from across the region to venues throughout the city. Today marks the first full day of organised competition across dozens of different sporting disciplines, with a formal opening ceremony featuring live entertainment scheduled for tomorrow evening at the Gold Coast Convention and Exhibition Centre. The biennial event brings together masters-level athletes typically aged thirty and above, with age-based competition categories allowing participants well into their seventies and eighties to continue competing in an organised, officiated environment across a huge range of sports. Local tourism operators are expecting a substantial economic boost from the event, with the influx of visiting competitors and their support networks filling hotels, restaurants, and local attractions during what's traditionally a comparatively quieter period on the city's events calendar. The games will continue running through to a closing ceremony scheduled for the fourteenth of November, with organisers describing recent days as largely devoted to finalising logistics across the many different venues hosting individual sporting events throughout the city.
