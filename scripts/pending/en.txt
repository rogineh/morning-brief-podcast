South Australia has launched the country's first Royal Commission into artificial intelligence, a wide-ranging inquiry that officials say will examine how AI is reshaping everything from the state's workforce to its public services, and what new regulatory guardrails, if any, might be needed. The commission is expected to hold public hearings and call witnesses from industry, government, and civil society over the coming months, and its findings could end up informing AI policy debates well beyond South Australia's own borders, given how few comprehensive, government-led reviews of this kind have been undertaken anywhere in the country so far. Supporters of the inquiry say it's an overdue step toward giving policymakers a clearer evidence base, rather than reacting piecemeal every time a new AI-related controversy makes headlines.

Meanwhile, SpaceX is reportedly looking to raise in the order of forty billion dollars, largely to fund the purchase of Nvidia AI chips to expand its own computing infrastructure. The scale of the raise underlines just how capital-intensive the race to build out AI computing capacity has become, with even a company as well-resourced as SpaceX needing to tap fresh outside funding to keep pace with the chip demands of frontier AI development.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

Construction has officially begun on the Nolans rare earths project in Central Australia, with a ground-breaking ceremony held at the site, about a hundred and thirty-five kilometres north of Alice Springs. The roughly one point eight billion dollar project, backed by both the Australian and United States governments as well as major private investors, is expected to take about three years to build and will ultimately produce neodymium and praseodymium, two rare earth elements used in everything from electric vehicle motors to wind turbines and defence equipment. The federal government says it's contributed more than a billion dollars in support through various critical minerals financing programs, and officials are hoping the project will supply a meaningful slice of global rare earths demand once it's up and running, reducing the country's reliance on imports for materials considered critical to the modern economy.

Skywatchers, meanwhile, are being told to keep an eye on the sky tonight, with forecasters expecting a solar flare's associated eruption to reach Earth and potentially stir up a moderate geomagnetic storm. If the forecast holds, it could bring aurora sightings further from the poles than usual, though space weather experts caution that the exact strength and timing of any resulting show always carries some uncertainty until the event is actually underway.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

The state's mining sector is watching closely after the High Court handed down a ruling this week in a case involving a proposed expansion of a Hunter Valley coal mine in New South Wales. The court's decision means that so-called scope three emissions, the emissions produced when exported coal is eventually burned overseas, must be properly assessed as part of the approval process for that project. While federal ministers have described the ruling as a fairly technical, New South Wales-specific decision, Queensland's resources sector, as the country's largest coal-producing industry, is worried it could still have flow-on effects for how major projects are assessed here. Premier David Crisafulli has sought to reassure industry, saying the state's existing environmental laws already account for this kind of consideration and that the government stands ready to make any legislative adjustments necessary if the ruling's implications turn out to reach further than expected.

Separately, the state government has opened a new round of expressions of interest, inviting resource companies to nominate areas they believe hold potential for future petroleum, gas, coal, and other mineral exploration. Officials say the process is designed to help identify the next generation of resource projects well before any formal land release, giving the government and local communities more time to weigh up the economic opportunities against any environmental and social concerns that might arise.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

Organisers of World Supercross have confirmed the rest of this year's season has been scrapped after just one round was completed, throwing the event's previously announced Gold Coast round, which had been scheduled for late November, into permanent doubt. The promoters say they're continuing discussions with potential new investors in the hope of relaunching the championship at some point, but for now, Gold Coast fans who'd been looking forward to the event will need to look elsewhere for their motorsport fix this summer.

In slightly stranger news, marine scientists say unusually large bluebottle jellyfish have been washing up on Gold Coast beaches in the lead-up to summer, sparking curiosity about whether their sting carries any more punch than that of their smaller, more familiar cousins. Researchers say they're still working to understand exactly how these larger specimens compare, and in the meantime, lifeguards are reminding beachgoers to steer clear of any bluebottles they spot on the sand, whatever their size.
