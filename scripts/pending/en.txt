In technology, a draft prospectus for Anthropic's planned initial public offering has revealed the scale of both the AI company's growth and its spending, according to reports reviewing the filing. Revenue for the first half of 2026 came in at sixteen point three billion dollars, nearly fifteen times higher than the same period a year earlier, while the company posted a net loss of eighty point seven billion dollars over that same six months. The vast majority of that loss wasn't from day-to-day operations, but from a large non-cash accounting charge tied to the changing value of convertible notes and other financing instruments the company has issued to raise capital. Spending on computing infrastructure climbed sharply too, rising to nearly nine billion dollars for the half, up from around two point three billion dollars a year earlier, as the company works through hundreds of billions of dollars in long-term cloud computing commitments with partners including Google and Amazon. The filing suggests an IPO could come as soon as next month, and analysts say the numbers illustrate just how capital-intensive the race to build and run large AI models has become, even for a company growing revenue at an extraordinary pace. The scale of the spending has reignited broader debate about whether current levels of investment across the AI industry are sustainable, or whether today's enormous infrastructure commitments are simply the cost of competing at the frontier of the technology. Should the listing proceed on the timeline suggested in the filing, it would rank among the largest technology offerings in years, putting fresh pressure on rival AI labs to demonstrate their own businesses can eventually translate rapid revenue growth into something closer to sustainable profitability.

Turning to national news, the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners has welcomed a federal government decision to expand GP training places, as part of a broader push to address doctor shortages in regional and remote communities. The college will receive an additional eighty seven Commonwealth-funded training places for 2028, bringing its total allocation to one thousand six hundred and twenty seven, with thirty seven of those new places specifically earmarked for rural training on top of the more than six hundred rural places already funded. The increase forms part of a national allocation of twenty one hundred GP training places for 2028 across all of the country's training providers. The college's president said the additional rural places were particularly welcome, given how disproportionately regional and remote communities continue to be affected by doctor shortages compared with major cities. Health policy groups have broadly welcomed the expansion but note that training more doctors is only part of the solution, with retention in rural posts once training is complete remaining an ongoing challenge that recent funding boosts alone haven't fully solved. Rural health advocates say incentives such as housing support and guaranteed city rotations for partners and families often matter as much to doctors' long-term decisions to stay in a region as the training pipeline itself.

In Queensland, the state government has kicked off the second round of its Games On grassroots sport infrastructure program, committing thirty million dollars to upgrade facilities at seventy two clubs across football, cricket, netball, and gymnastics. The funding is split between twenty million dollars for smaller community projects covering things like courts, change rooms, and lighting, and ten million dollars for a separate partnership fund supporting larger upgrades. The University of Queensland Football Club at Chapel Hill has been named as the first confirmed recipient under this round, receiving more than one point four million dollars toward a new clubhouse. The program is being framed by the state government as part of the broader sporting legacy build-up ahead of the Brisbane 2032 Olympics, with the first round of funding having already supported well over a hundred projects, a significant portion of which are now complete or under construction. Local sporting clubs have welcomed the funding as a rare chance to tackle long-deferred maintenance and upgrade work that membership fees and fundraising alone often can't cover, and several clubs say the certainty of a scheduled second funding round has made it easier to plan upgrades in stages rather than chase one-off grants as they appear.

And on the Gold Coast, police and Queensland's Office of Fair Trading have stepped up a joint crackdown on non-compliant e-bikes and e-scooters, months after new road rules for e-mobility devices took effect in July. Police say they've seized hundreds of non-compliant devices and issued close to three thousand penalties for road rule breaches, including cases involving drink driving, since the new laws came into force. The Office of Fair Trading has separately begun visiting local retailers to check that e-bikes being advertised and sold as road-legal genuinely meet the required standards, with investigations into at least two Gold Coast retailers already resulting in fines for non-compliance. Authorities say the crackdown reflects growing concern about the safety of poorly regulated, high-powered e-bikes being used on footpaths and roads, particularly among younger riders, after a string of earlier incidents involving e-bikes travelling at highway speeds. Local parents' groups have broadly welcomed the stronger enforcement, while some retailers say clearer guidance earlier would have helped them stay compliant without the threat of fines after the fact.
