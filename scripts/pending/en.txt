Nvidia-backed AI data centre operator Firmus has shelved its planned five billion dollar initial public offering, citing market volatility and saying the terms on offer simply didn't reflect the value of its business. Reports suggest the float had struggled to attract sufficient investor demand even after bankers floated cutting the company's valuation by as much as a third, and Firmus says it will instead pursue a private fundraising round. The episode has been read by some analysts as an early sign of investors applying more scrutiny to AI infrastructure valuations, after a period in which eye-watering price tags attracted comparatively little pushback. Firmus had been seeking a valuation north of thirty billion dollars despite relatively modest current revenue, a gap that some prospective investors reportedly found difficult to justify even accounting for the enormous future data centre build-out the company has planned. The company says it remains committed to its expansion plans regardless of how it ultimately raises the capital to fund them.

On the product side, cloud storage company Scality has launched what it's calling an AI inference factory, a package designed to let large enterprises run AI models on their own premises rather than relying entirely on public cloud providers. The company says the push reflects growing demand from businesses in sectors like finance and healthcare, where data sensitivity or regulatory requirements make it harder to justify sending everything out to third-party AI infrastructure. Executives say customers increasingly want the flexibility of running AI workloads locally during peak periods while still being able to burst into the public cloud when extra capacity is needed, rather than being locked into one model or the other.

Australia's women's cricket team posted a commanding seven-wicket win over Bangladesh in the first one-day international in Brisbane, with opener Phoebe Litchfield anchoring the chase with a quick half-century. The result gets the home side's one-day campaign off to a strong start, and selectors will be hoping for more of the same consistency as the series continues. Litchfield's innings was built on crisp timing through the off side rather than outright power, and teammates praised her ability to rotate the strike and keep the required run rate comfortably in check throughout the chase.

Elsewhere, more than sixty-five thousand Canberrans are currently on waiting lists to see a medical specialist through the public system, according to figures that have prompted Canberra Health Services to promise a formal review of how those waitlists are being managed. Patient advocacy groups say the numbers reflect a broader, national pattern of specialist access lagging well behind demand, particularly outside the major capital cities, and they're calling for the review to look closely at whether some conditions could be managed through alternative pathways while people wait. Canberra Health Services says it recognises the frustration the delays are causing and has promised to publish clearer information on expected wait times by specialty, so patients have a better sense of what to expect when they're first referred.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

The latest state health performance data shows ambulance ramping, the practice of paramedics waiting with patients outside emergency departments because there's no bed available inside, sitting at around forty-two per cent across the state's twenty-six busiest public hospitals. The figures have reignited debate over whether the government's promised improvements to emergency department wait times are being delivered quickly enough, with health unions arguing that without more hospital beds and staff, ramping figures are unlikely to improve in any meaningful way. The state government points to its ongoing Hospital Rescue Plan and the thousands of additional beds it says are being delivered under that program as evidence that relief is on the way, even if it's not yet showing up clearly in the week-to-week figures.

Separately, a youth justice watchdog has raised concerns that a watch house originally built to hold adult prisoners may still be needed to house children, depending on how proposed bail and sentencing changes play out once they take effect. The watchdog's report focuses on capacity planning rather than individual cases, warning that without adequate purpose-built youth facilities in place, authorities could be left relying on unsuitable adult infrastructure for longer than intended. The government says it's continuing to invest in dedicated youth detention capacity, but acknowledges that building new facilities takes time, and that managing demand in the interim remains an ongoing challenge.
