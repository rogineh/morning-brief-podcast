Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Friday, the 30th of October, 2026. Here's what's happening today in technology and AI, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, OpenAI has announced that eligible ChatGPT Pro and Business subscribers can now claim up to five hundred dollars a month in free credits toward the company's separate developer API, in a move some analysts have described as an indirect price cut aimed at keeping power users firmly inside OpenAI's own ecosystem. Under the offer, Pro subscribers receive credits matching their monthly subscription cost, while Business customers get credits pooled across their whole workspace, calculated per user seat and capped at five hundred dollars overall. To actually claim the credits, users need to link their ChatGPT billing account to an API organisation they personally own, with the account required to have been active for at least a week beforehand, and eligibility varying somewhat depending on which country a subscriber is based in. Free trial users, along with certain enterprise and education tier subscribers, aren't eligible for the offer, and any unused credits don't roll over, expiring at the end of each billing cycle rather than accumulating over time. The move comes as competition between major AI companies to lock in loyal, high-spending users continues to intensify, with firms increasingly using credits, bundling, and cross-product incentives rather than straightforward price cuts to keep customers engaged across their wider product ecosystems.

Turning to national news, the union representing Commonwealth public servants is urging a measured, carefully scrutinised rollout of artificial intelligence across government agencies, ahead of its national secretary fronting a federal parliamentary inquiry into AI use in the public sector today. In its submission to the inquiry, the Community and Public Sector Union argues that the push for efficiency gains through automation must not come at the expense of lawful, accurate decision-making or reliable public services for everyday citizens. The union is separately pushing for specific AI-related protections to be written directly into public servants' workplace agreements, including clear limits on using AI tools for staff surveillance, recruitment decisions, performance assessments, or promotion processes. The federal government has so far resisted the union's push for a standalone, dedicated AI clause in enterprise bargaining agreements, with the minister overseeing public service matters saying negotiations will continue in good faith, while officials have separately argued that overly prescriptive rules risk becoming outdated almost as quickly as they're written given how fast AI technology continues to change. Separate reporting has also raised questions about oversight, noting that government agencies' own public accounts of how they're using AI internally remain largely self-reported and not independently verified by any outside body.

In Queensland, the state government's contentious breach bail laws remain under intense scrutiny as they move through parliament, with victim advocates broadly welcoming the tougher approach while legal groups warn the changes risk further overloading an already strained court system. Under the proposed laws, described by the Premier as the strongest bail provisions in the country, anyone who commits a serious offence while already on bail would face a mandatory minimum sentence of twelve months behind bars, with courts required to be satisfied to a high degree of confidence that an accused person won't reoffend before granting bail on serious charges. The state's Bar Association and Law Society have both raised concerns during parliamentary hearings, arguing the proposed "high degree of confidence" legal test has no clearly established meaning in existing criminal law and could create genuine uncertainty for courts trying to apply it consistently. More than two hundred and fifty submissions were made to the parliamentary committee reviewing the bill, with many raising concerns about the pressure the changes could place on an already overburdened justice and prison system, even as the government has separately conceded the laws will likely lead to more people being held behind bars overall. The government has committed to passing the legislation by the end of the year regardless, having already indicated it's prepared to override standard human rights protections to get the changes through parliament.

And on the Gold Coast, the AFLW Suns have booked their first finals appearance since 2023 after surging up the ladder into fourth position, but now face genuine uncertainty over where they'll actually play their home finals matches given their usual ground is unavailable. Carrara Stadium, the Suns' regular home venue, is booked out for weeks two, three, and four of the finals series to host an international Twenty20 cricket match between Australia and England, leaving the club and the AFL scrambling to lock in an alternative venue in time. Among the options reportedly being considered is a ground an hour's drive up the highway in Springfield, shared with Brisbane Lions matches, along with a smaller suburban venue on the Gold Coast itself, though the latter would require any final to be played during the day given it lacks suitable floodlighting. A Suns club spokesperson says the organisation is working closely with all relevant stakeholders to secure appropriate home venues for each week Carrara remains unavailable throughout the finals series. One scenario could still work firmly in the Suns' favour, since a win in their final home and away match this weekend would lock in a double chance and guarantee the club hosts a home final somewhere during the finals series, regardless of which specific venue that ultimately turns out to be.
