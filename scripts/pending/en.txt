Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Thursday, the 17th of September, 2026. Here's what's happening today across technology, the nation, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

First, technology and AI. In a rare show of coordination among rival labs, Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI have jointly unveiled new cyber AI models, safeguards, and access programs aimed at using artificial intelligence defensively against the same kinds of attacks it could otherwise be used to carry out. The move comes as all three companies face growing pressure to demonstrate their systems can be trusted with sensitive security work, rather than simply generating headlines about their offensive potential, and follows months of warnings from security researchers that the same models capable of finding and patching vulnerabilities could just as easily be turned toward discovering and exploiting them at scale. Separately, OpenAI has begun piloting what it calls Sponsored Agents inside ChatGPT, a feature that lets users interact with a clearly labelled, advertiser-run conversational agent rather than the company's own assistant, marking one of the clearest signs yet of how the company intends to eventually monetise its enormous user base beyond subscriptions. The pilot is limited for now, but analysts say it points to advertising becoming a permanent fixture of mainstream AI products in the years ahead, following a path well worn by search engines and social media before it, even as it raises fresh questions about how clearly users will be able to tell a sponsored response apart from the assistant's own independent answer.

Now to national news here in Australia. Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke is addressing the National Press Club today to formally unveil Labor's long-delayed overhaul of the immigration system, more than a month after the speech was abruptly postponed amid disagreement between ministers. The plan, now endorsed by Cabinet's Expenditure Review Committee, is expected to confirm that international students will effectively be barred from bringing partners and children to Australia on secondary visas, as part of a broader push to bring net overseas migration down from around three hundred thousand to two hundred and twenty-five thousand by 2028. The speech, titled around the question of who arrives, who stays, and who leaves, is also expected to touch on tighter rules for asylum seeker work rights and appeals, and limits on how long people can stay on backpacker visas. The announcement comes as separate figures show backpacker numbers in Australia have hit an all-time high, with working holiday makers now accounting for more than half of the country's net overseas migration figure, prompting the government to slow visa processing for applicants from two dozen countries as it looks for ways to bring the overall numbers down without shutting the program off altogether. Tourism and regional business groups have warned any sharp cut could hit hard in the farming and hospitality sectors that have come to rely heavily on backpacker labour, particularly outside the major capital cities.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. A Mary Valley farming family that feared an eighty-year dairying legacy was about to come to an end has been thrown a last-minute lifeline. The Rozynski family lost its milk supply contract with Maleny Dairies back in June and faced the prospect of culling its prized herd of six hundred Brown Swiss cows, but Kenilworth Dairies has now stepped in to buy and bottle a share of their daily milk production under a new premium, single-origin brand built specifically around the family's herd. The deal is being welcomed across the region's dairy industry as a rare good-news story after years of contraction and factory closures that have put pressure on small producers right across south-east Queensland, with industry groups saying it shows there's still a viable path forward for family operations willing to specialise rather than compete purely on volume.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. Mayor Tom Tate remains under pressure over his claim that he shared a private dinner of tomahawk steaks and expensive wine with President Trump at Mar-a-Lago earlier this year, after the Trump Organisation described his account as, in its words, a complete fabrication, saying the president merely said a brief hello to the mayor's table while on the patio. Tate has doubled down on his version of events, insisting he discussed tariffs with the president shortly before the dinner, even as a right-to-information search of council records turned up no evidence of a formal invitation, with his office instead saying the invitation was issued verbally rather than in writing. The saga has taken an awkward turn in recent days, with Tate storming out of a press conference and later apologising publicly after swearing at a journalist who pressed him on the inconsistencies in his story, an episode that has drawn almost as much attention locally as the original dinner claim itself.
