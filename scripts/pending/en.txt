Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Thursday, the 15th of October, 2026. Here's what you need to know today, starting with technology and AI.

The soaring electricity demands of artificial intelligence are putting growing strain on power grids around the world, with new analysis suggesting data centre electricity use could nearly triple within the next couple of years as AI workloads continue to scale up. Some forecasts now put data centres on track to consume close to a tenth of total electricity in markets like the United States by the end of the decade, up from a small fraction just a few years ago. Utility companies say they're increasingly struggling to predict exactly when and where the next big spike in demand will land, and grid operators in several regions are already warning that available capacity could fall well short of what's needed within the next decade unless new generation and transmission infrastructure comes online fast enough. Industry figures argue the answer isn't to slow AI development, but to accelerate investment in energy infrastructure alongside it, while critics counter that the industry has been slow to confront just how resource intensive its own growth has become. One widely cited comparison suggests a single AI chatbot query can use roughly ten times the electricity of an equivalent search engine request, a gap that becomes significant once multiplied across billions of queries a day.

Separately, Anthropic says it's significantly expanding its international footprint, with plans to roughly triple the size of its workforce outside the United States and substantially grow the team working on applied AI projects for business customers. The expansion reflects a broader trend among the leading AI labs of racing to build out global sales, support, and engineering operations as demand for enterprise AI tools continues to climb well beyond where it stood just a year or two ago. Analysts say the scale of the build-out also underlines how much the major labs are now competing on execution and distribution, not just on the underlying capability of their models, since even the most advanced model is of limited commercial value if a company can't get it in front of enterprise customers and support them once it's deployed.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The federal government has used this year's Land Forces exposition in Perth to outline six technology priorities it says will shape the next decade of defence spending, including long range and hypersonic weapons, high energy lasers, autonomous systems, quantum technology, and artificial intelligence. The announcement forms part of a broader plan to spend some four hundred and twenty five billion dollars on defence over the next ten years, with officials framing the priorities as an effort to keep pace with rapidly evolving military technology rather than investing solely in traditional platforms. Defence analysts say the heavy emphasis on autonomous systems and AI in particular reflects lessons drawn from recent conflicts overseas, where uncrewed systems have played a far larger role than many military planners anticipated only a few years ago. Industry groups have broadly welcomed the strategy, though some have cautioned that translating these priorities into working capability on realistic timelines will be the real test, given how often large defence procurement programs have historically run over budget and behind schedule. The government says it's hoping to avoid those pitfalls by favouring smaller, more flexible contracts for emerging technology areas rather than committing early to a handful of massive, slow-moving platforms.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

The state government has taken its community cabinet process to Goondiwindi, with the Premier and a number of his ministers fielding questions directly from around one hundred and fifty local residents on issues ranging from regional infrastructure to health and education services. Organisers say the format is designed to give rural and regional communities a more direct line to decision makers than they'd typically get through standard government channels, and this year's event was the third community cabinet held so far in 2026. Local community leaders say the sessions give residents a rare chance to raise hyper-local concerns directly with the people who control the relevant budgets, even if not every issue raised can realistically be resolved on the spot. The government says it plans to continue holding similar sessions in different regional centres throughout the remainder of the year, framing the approach as a core part of how it wants to engage with communities outside the south east corner of the state.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The City of Gold Coast is seeking a private development partner to help redevelop a nearly eight hectare former administration site in Nerang, with council hoping the site can be transformed into a mixed use precinct combining new housing with community facilities. The project forms part of the council's broader land for infrastructure model, under which surplus council land is used to help fund public amenities elsewhere in the city rather than simply being sold off outright. Separately, a long running legal dispute over a proposed beachfront tower at Bilinga has taken another turn, after a court decision overturned the council's earlier approval of extra building height for the project. The council is now working to protect a broader development pipeline worth some five and a half billion dollars while it waits to learn whether a further appeal will be heard, in a case that's become a closely watched test of how far councils can stretch height limits for major coastal developments.
