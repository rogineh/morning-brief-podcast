Both OpenAI and Anthropic are reportedly moving ahead with plans for stock market listings, after each company is said to have confidentially filed paperwork with securities regulators in recent months. If they proceed, the listings would rank among the largest the technology industry has ever seen, reflecting just how much capital the leading AI labs believe they'll need to keep training ever larger models and building out data centre capacity. Neither company has formally confirmed a timeline, and reporting on exactly when any listing might happen has been inconsistent, but the mere fact that both of the industry's best-funded labs are preparing the groundwork is being read as a sign that investors are keen for a more direct way to back the AI boom beyond simply buying shares in the big cloud providers. Analysts note that a successful listing from either company would also set a benchmark valuation for the rest of the sector, potentially reshaping how investors price every other AI startup still raising money privately.

Separately, Anthropic says it has identified and shut down a number of attempts to misuse its Claude models for malicious purposes in recent months, including efforts tied to cyber operations and surveillance activity, as part of a broader threat intelligence report the company published. The company said its own systems flagged the suspicious behaviour before it could cause serious harm, and used the cases to argue that AI developers need to keep investing heavily in abuse detection as these tools become more capable and more widely available. Security researchers broadly welcomed the disclosure as a useful window into real-world misuse patterns, though some noted that the same report highlights just how creative bad actors are already becoming in testing the limits of what these systems will do. The company said it would keep publishing similar findings periodically, arguing that transparency about misuse attempts, rather than silence, is what will ultimately build public trust in the technology.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

The National Disability Insurance Scheme's reform rollout is entering a new phase this month, with staged implementation of revised support arrangements beginning this October and continuing through to next September. The changes include standardised, evidence-based assessments of participants' functional capacity, revised arrangements for plan renewals, and new rules affecting people with the highest support needs, as the government looks to slow the scheme's rapid cost growth without unwinding its core guarantees. Disability advocates have broadly welcomed the staged approach as preferable to a sudden switch-over, though several groups are still pressing the government for more clarity on exactly how individual participants' existing plans will be affected as the new framework phases in over the coming year. The government says the reforms sit alongside a separate multi-billion dollar aged care funding package announced earlier this year, which it describes as a parallel effort to put both systems on a more sustainable long-term footing.

Now, the news from here in Queensland.

The state government has named the first five of twenty two new schools planned for fast-growing parts of southern Queensland, with foundation students set to start at each site from the beginning of term one in 2027. The announcement forms part of a record education budget, and the Education Minister says the new schools are designed to get ahead of population growth in outer suburban growth corridors rather than scrambling to catch up once local schools are already over capacity. Separately, construction has begun on a ninety eight million dollar primary school on the Sunshine Coast, with capacity for more than eleven hundred students once it opens its doors in term one of 2028, as the government continues a broader wave of new school construction across the state's fastest growing regions. Teachers' groups have welcomed the investment but say it needs to be matched by a renewed push on staffing, warning that new buildings alone won't ease workload pressures if classrooms can't be adequately staffed once the doors open.

And finally, here's what's happening on the Gold Coast.

The state government has unveiled plans for two new metro-style bus routes linking Broadbeach to Nerang and Miami to Robina, running as part of a broader package of road upgrades and new stations designed to ease congestion on some of the city's busiest east-west corridors. Planners say the government will also preserve the rail corridor between Varsity Lakes and the Gold Coast Airport, keeping the door open for a future heavy rail extension even though that project isn't funded as part of this current package. The announcement comes alongside a wider twenty year Gold Coast transport plan that includes the next stage of the Coomera Connector between Pimpama and Coomera, with expressions of interest for that project's design now open, plus upgrades to several Pacific Motorway interchanges as the city continues preparing its transport network for sustained population growth and the lead-up to the 2032 Olympics. Local business groups have welcomed the scale of the investment, though some residents along the affected corridors say they're still waiting on clearer detail about construction timelines and how disruption will be managed once work gets underway.
