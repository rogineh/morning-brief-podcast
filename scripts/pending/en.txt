Good morning, and welcome to your Daily Brief for Wednesday, the 28th of October, 2026. Here's what's happening today in technology and AI, across Australia, Queensland, and the Gold Coast.

In technology, Google DeepMind has unveiled Genie 4, a new interactive world model capable of generating detailed, explorable virtual environments complete with native audio, including ambient sound, music, and even dialogue generated directly alongside the visuals. The system allows users to steer a simulation in real time using simple text prompts, letting them change the weather, move the camera, or introduce new characters mid-scene, while maintaining a consistent, coherent environment for up to two minutes, roughly double what the previous version could manage. Access for now is limited to a research preview, available to trusted testers, subscribers on Google's higher-tier AI plans through a program called Project Genie, and selected early-access partners working with Google's developer tools. DeepMind says every piece of generated video and audio carries an invisible digital watermark to help identify AI-generated content, and that the system underwent internal safety evaluations before being released even in this limited preview form. The company is positioning the technology as a meaningful step toward more general artificial intelligence, with potential uses ranging from safe virtual training environments for robots and software agents through to creative tools for filmmakers, game designers, and other industries looking to prototype entire virtual worlds without traditional production pipelines.

Turning to national news, Greens Senator Jordon Steele-John has stepped back from the party room and his usual parliamentary duties while an independent process examines allegations of inappropriate conduct made against him by several party members. Reports indicate a group of Greens members wrote to party leader David Shoebridge outlining concerns about conduct dating back several years, prompting the senator to step aside voluntarily while the claims are properly examined through a process run by the Western Australian branch of the party. Steele-John has publicly rejected the allegations, describing the initial complaint as unspecific, while saying he remains willing to respond to any specific claim once it's properly put to him. The Greens leadership has described the matter as being taken incredibly seriously, with the stepping aside characterised as a procedural safeguard during the review process rather than any finding of wrongdoing. The episode has reportedly created some public friction within the party itself, highlighting the broader challenges political organisations face in balancing a fair process for the person accused against the need to take complaints from their own members seriously and transparently.

In Queensland, mining companies across the state's north-west are warning that a persistent shortage of skilled workers threatens to slow a wave of new project activity, even as roughly eighty five critical minerals projects progress through various stages of drilling, exploration, and development across the region. Industry figures say finding staff has become difficult almost everywhere in the sector, with one mining company executive noting a strong preference for hiring locally before turning to fly-in fly-out arrangements bringing workers in from regional hubs like Townsville and Brisbane. Nationally, the resources sector is projected to need an additional twenty four thousand workers by 2027 to deliver already-planned projects, with mining engineers, geologists, electricians, and mechanical engineers identified as the hardest roles to fill, partly due to competition from major infrastructure projects elsewhere in the state. The state government has committed close to one hundred and sixty million dollars this financial year toward subsidised vocational training places aimed specifically at the resources sector, as part of a broader workforce plan containing nearly thirty targeted actions. Industry representatives have welcomed the funding but caution it will likely take several years for a meaningful new generation of skilled workers to actually flow through into the industry, meaning the shortage is likely to remain a binding constraint on project timelines for some time yet.

And on the Gold Coast, the Planning and Environment Court has overturned council approval for a fourteen storey, one hundred and thirty million dollar tower at Kirra, ruling that its design simply didn't justify breaching the area's height limit by fifty per cent. The presiding judge described the proposed tower as visually jarring and said it would create a canyon-like effect for nearby residents, with both the council and the project's developer having already conceded the design didn't technically comply with standard planning limits for the site. The approved development would have included more than three hundred bedrooms, close to eight times the density typically allowed under the council's own planning rules for that part of Kirra, prompting the successful legal challenge from residents who argued the project simply didn't belong at that scale in a predominantly low-rise beachside suburb. The ruling follows a separate, related court decision in which a different Gold Coast high-rise approval was also struck down, after judges found arguments about improving housing choice and affordability didn't adequately justify exceeding standard height limits in that case either. The council is currently pursuing a separate appeal to the High Court over that earlier ruling, meaning the broader legal fight over how far councils can stretch planning rules to approve taller buildings on the Gold Coast looks far from settled.
