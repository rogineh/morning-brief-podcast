In technology, Meta has indefinitely paused a controversial update to its commercial terms of service that would have allowed the company to scrape websites belonging to its Business account holders, after facing sustained criticism from publishers and regulators around the world. The update, which had been due to take effect later this month, originally included language permitting Meta to use scraped content to help train its artificial intelligence systems, though the company has since removed that specific wording and says any future version would limit use strictly to advertising and other business products. Meta's communications team says the pause reflects a genuine effort to get the policy right after hearing extensive feedback from publishers, though critics remain concerned the underlying intent could resurface in a revised form. The pressure campaign against the update had been building on multiple fronts, with courts in Norway and Brazil issuing preliminary injunctions blocking the terms, Britain's competition regulator opening a formal investigation, and a bipartisan pair of American senators publicly calling on the company to withdraw the proposal altogether. Meta says it will provide a further update once it has more to share, though no new timeline or effective date has yet been announced for any revised version of the policy.

Turning to national news, the Reserve Bank's detailed economic forecasts released as part of its November Statement on Monetary Policy point to a bumpier road ahead than many had hoped, with the central bank now expecting headline inflation to peak at around four and a half per cent before remaining above its target band all the way through to late next year. The statement follows the board's decision earlier this month to lift the official cash rate for a fifth time this year, and officials say the updated forecasts reflect a number of upside risks to inflation that have materialised more forcefully than anticipated when earlier projections were drawn up. The central bank has stopped short of explicitly signalling further rate increases are coming, instead describing its current stance as appropriately balanced given the considerable uncertainty still surrounding both the inflation outlook and global economic conditions more broadly. Economists have offered mixed reactions to the detailed forecasts, with some arguing the central bank may still be underestimating how long elevated price pressures will persist, while others say the overall trajectory described remains broadly consistent with inflation eventually returning to target over a reasonable timeframe. Households already grappling with higher mortgage repayments following this year's rate rises are being told to expect little near-term relief on the cost-of-living front based on the bank's own updated outlook.

In Queensland, a bill currently before a parliamentary committee would remove longstanding requirements forcing workers at the Byerwen coal mine in the state's north to live in the nearby town of Glenden, reversing a measure introduced by the previous state government several years ago. The original legislation had been designed to protect Glenden's viability as a functioning community by requiring the mine's operator to house its workforce locally rather than relying heavily on fly-in, fly-out arrangements that can leave small mining towns increasingly hollowed out over time. The committee reviewing the proposed changes received public submissions last month and was due to hand down its report on the bill earlier this month, though its findings don't appear to have been made public just yet. Supporters of repealing the requirement argue it imposes unnecessary and inflexible constraints on how mining companies manage their workforce, while community advocates in the region have previously fought hard to keep the protections in place, crediting them with helping sustain the town through difficult periods. The debate touches on a broader and recurring tension across Queensland's mining regions between operational flexibility for resource companies and the long-term sustainability of the communities that have grown up around individual mine sites.

And on the Gold Coast, Australia's first footpath delivery robots have begun operating around Griffith University's Gold Coast campus, marking the first time the American company behind the technology has launched its service anywhere outside the United States. The small, autonomous delivery robots are based out of an operations depot located at the university itself, making Griffith the first university anywhere to host this kind of robotics operation on its own grounds. A well-known local restaurant was the first to come on board as a delivery partner, with additional eateries expected to join the service in the coming weeks as the rollout continues to expand based on early community feedback. The American company behind the robots has flagged plans to invest roughly half a million dollars in the local region over the coming year, and says it's already exploring internship and early career opportunities for Griffith students interested in robotics, autonomous systems, and field engineering. University officials say hosting the operations depot gives students a rare hands-on window into how a genuinely novel piece of consumer technology actually gets deployed and managed in the real world, rather than simply studying it in theory. Local customers can choose whether or not to opt into having their orders delivered by robot rather than a traditional human driver.
