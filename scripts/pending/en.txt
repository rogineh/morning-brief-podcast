In technology, cloud computing firm CoreWeave has become the latest flashpoint in growing investor unease over how the AI boom is being financed. The company's shares sank to a fresh 52-week low this week as concerns deepened over its more than fifty billion dollars in debt taken on to build out AI data centres, with credit default swap spreads on that debt reaching distressed levels. HSBC maintained a bearish rating on the stock, warning that rising borrowing costs and any slowdown in demand for AI computing power could hurt the company's long-term profitability, and setting a price target well below where shares currently trade. Not every analyst is as pessimistic. Goldman Sachs upgraded CoreWeave to a buy rating, arguing that investors are overestimating the company's future cost of capital and underestimating the potential returns on its investments, while other analysts point out that much of the company's debt is backed by long-term contracts with major customers including Meta, Microsoft, and OpenAI. Adding to the unease, CoreWeave's three co-founders have sold billions of dollars worth of stock since the company's 2025 listing, a pattern some investors read as a lack of confidence even as the business continues to expand. The episode is being watched closely as a test case for whether the enormous borrowing underpinning the broader AI infrastructure build-out is sustainable, or whether it's building up risk that could eventually spill over into wider markets. Similar debt-fuelled buildouts are underway at other so-called neocloud providers racing to supply the computing power major AI labs say they need, meaning CoreWeave's troubles are being read by some analysts as an early signal for the sector as a whole rather than a problem unique to one company.

Turning to national news, National Australia Bank has announced plans to cut around two and a half thousand jobs, roughly six per cent of its workforce, by the middle of 2027, in a move analysts say reflects deeper pressure across the broader economy rather than problems specific to the bank itself. The announcement sent NAB shares lower and comes against a backdrop of weak national productivity, with gross domestic product per hour worked falling zero point two per cent over the year to June. Economists have pointed out that the Reserve Bank has already been forced to raise interest rates four times this year in an effort to tame inflation, and that further large-scale job cuts at big companies risk weakening consumer spending and economic growth just as households are already absorbing higher borrowing costs. Separately, the Australian dollar firmed on hawkish comments from a Reserve Bank official alongside stronger than expected economic growth data out of China, a reminder that the local economy remains closely tied to conditions in its largest trading partner even as domestic pressures mount. Unions representing bank workers have called on NAB to prioritise redeployment and retraining over outright redundancies wherever possible, while the bank maintains the cuts are necessary to keep its cost base competitive as more routine functions move to automated and digital systems.

In Queensland, James Cook University has confirmed it's establishing a new Centre for Climate Adaptation and Disaster Resilience, aimed at driving innovation and coordinated action across disaster preparedness, response, and recovery for communities in the state's north and beyond. university leadership says the centre will draw together expertise from engineering, ecology, public health, social science, policy, and Indigenous knowledge, reflecting a push to treat disaster resilience as something that needs input from many different fields rather than a single agency or discipline working in isolation. Vice-Chancellor Simon Biggs described the move as a significant investment in expanding the university's capability in an area of growing importance, as communities across tropical and regional Queensland continue to face more frequent and severe weather events. Further details on the centre's specific programs and research priorities are expected to be released as it formally stands up its operations in the coming months, with local councils and emergency services agencies already flagged as early partners the university hopes to work alongside.

And on the Gold Coast, pressure is building on the state government over calls to extend the city's heavy rail line south from Varsity Lakes to Gold Coast Airport at Coolangatta, after a federal Liberal National Party member said an online survey of his found strong local support for the project. Queensland's Transport Minister, Brent Mickelberg, responded to a parliamentary petition by confirming the extension isn't currently part of the state's Gold Coast transport plan, though he said the government is preserving the rail corridor from Varsity Lakes through to the airport to allow for a future upgrade if one is ever funded. The stance leaves residents and businesses along the southern Gold Coast in a familiar holding pattern, with the corridor kept open in principle but no timeline or funding commitment in place. It comes as the state's broader transport plan for the city presses ahead with other projects, including a metro-style bus service, after a separate and more contentious light rail extension toward the airport was shelved last year. Local business groups along the proposed heavy rail corridor say certainty, either way, would be preferable to the current open-ended holding pattern, which makes it harder for them to plan around any future construction disruption or property changes.
