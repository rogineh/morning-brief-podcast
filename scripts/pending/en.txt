First, technology and AI. OpenAI has launched two new models, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna, priced at roughly half the rate of its previous top-tier offering, a move widely read as a direct response to intensifying price competition from rivals racing to make frontier-level performance more affordable for everyday developers and businesses. The launch comes in the same week that Chinese manufacturer Xiaomi released its own new family of models, including a large omnimodal system and a smaller, more efficient variant designed to run with a very long context window, underlining just how quickly capable AI models are proliferating well beyond the handful of companies that dominated the space only a year or two ago. Analysts say the combination of falling prices and rapidly multiplying model choices is beginning to reshape how businesses budget for AI tools, with some warning that the sheer pace of new releases makes it increasingly difficult for smaller companies to know which models are worth building around for the long term rather than chasing whichever option is cheapest this month. Industry watchers say the price war shows little sign of slowing, with each new release from a major lab typically prompting a round of matching cuts from competitors within days, a dynamic that's proving good for developers in the short term even as it squeezes margins across the industry.

Now to national news here in Australia. A royal commission into corruption in Victoria's construction sector has opened its first public hearing in Melbourne, with the presiding commissioner issuing a blunt warning to "all people who have done wrong" that the inquiry intends to get to the bottom of misconduct on major public infrastructure projects. Allegations to be examined include claims that construction union officials threatened industrial action to force employers into paying inflated wages to unqualified associates of union bosses, with two blocks of hearings scheduled before the end of the year and a final report due to the state government by next August. Separately, authorities are warning congregations across the country about a scam in which fraudsters impersonate church ministers by email, then ask parishioners to purchase gift cards on behalf of a supposedly vulnerable member of the community, a con that's been running nationally since 2021 but continues to catch out well-meaning churchgoers, particularly those less familiar with recognising phishing attempts. Consumer protection officials say the scam has proven remarkably durable precisely because it exploits trust and generosity rather than greed, and are urging church communities to verify any unusual request by phone before acting on it, no matter how convincing the email appears.

Closer to home, here in Queensland. The state government has expanded its Adult Crime, Adult Time laws to cover a total of forty-five offences, adding twelve new categories including domestic violence strangulation, riot, conspiring to murder, and indecent treatment of a child, in the latest tranche of a policy the government says is aimed at making the state safer and reducing the number of victims of serious youth crime. The Youth Justice Minister says the expanded list builds on earlier changes that have already seen a meaningful drop in offending under the scheme, though critics of the approach continue to argue that treating children as adults in the justice system risks entrenching disadvantage rather than addressing its root causes. In more encouraging health news, the latest quarterly data shows ambulance ramping outside Queensland hospitals has fallen to its lowest level in five years, a result health authorities are crediting to a mix of extra hospital capacity and process improvements aimed at getting patients off ambulance stretchers and into emergency departments more quickly. Paramedic representatives have welcomed the improvement but caution that ramping pressures tend to worsen again over the summer months, and are calling for the gains to be locked in with continued investment rather than treated as a one-off result.

And finally, your Gold Coast update. Construction giant Multiplex has been formally appointed as managing contractor to build the new Coomera Hospital, a major public health project that will eventually deliver at least six hundred beds to serve the fast-growing northern Gold Coast. Main construction work is set to begin in October, with the first stage of four hundred beds and key services including an emergency department, operating theatres, maternity care, and mental health inpatient units expected to open by 2031, followed by a second stage adding a further two hundred beds along with services such as renal dialysis, oncology, and palliative care the following year. The project's projected cost has climbed from an original one point three billion dollars to two point two five billion dollars amid broader construction cost pressures, and the timeline has slipped from an initial 2027 opening, but state health officials say the expanded scope reflects just how much the northern Gold Coast's population has grown since the hospital was first planned, and that the investment will make a substantial long-term difference to healthcare access in the region.
