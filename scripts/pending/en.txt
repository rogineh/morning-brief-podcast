Separately, Microsoft says it's achieved a significant improvement in quantum error correction, reporting a logical error rate roughly eight hundred times lower than the error rate of the underlying physical qubits used to build it. Researchers say reducing errors at this scale is one of the central challenges standing between today's experimental quantum machines and genuinely useful, large-scale quantum computers capable of tackling problems classical computers can't handle. The company frames the result as a meaningful step along that path rather than a finished solution, with engineers cautioning that substantially more error correction progress, alongside hardware improvements, will still be needed before quantum computing moves from the research lab into everyday commercial use. Even so, researchers in the field say the pace of improvement in error rates over the past few years has been faster than many predicted, fuelling renewed optimism that genuinely useful quantum machines could arrive on a shorter timeline than the decade-plus horizon that's often been cited.

Turning now to news from across Australia.

New figures show more Australians are moving from major cities to regional areas than at any point in recent memory, with the latest quarterly measure of regional population movement reaching its highest level on record. Analysts say the trend reflects a combination of factors, including the continued availability of flexible and remote work arrangements, the relatively higher cost of capital city housing, and a broader lifestyle shift that accelerated during the pandemic and has proven more durable than many expected. Regional business groups have welcomed the influx as a boost to local economies and housing demand in many towns, though some regional mayors caution that infrastructure, including schools, healthcare, and roads, needs to keep pace with population growth rather than lagging behind it as new residents arrive faster than services can be expanded to meet them. Real estate agents in several popular regional centres say buyer competition has intensified noticeably over the past year, pushing prices up in towns that were once considered comfortably affordable alternatives to capital city living.
