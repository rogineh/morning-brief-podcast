In technology, SK Hyper, the AI data centre arm of South Korean telecommunications giant SK Telecom, is seeking around three hundred and fifty billion dollars in outside capital to fund the first phase of an enormous planned AI data centre buildout. The company's chief executive said it's in talks with major technology firms and financial investors to secure funding for an initial five gigawatt phase, which forms part of a broader plan to eventually reach fifteen gigawatts of AI computing capacity inside South Korea by 2035. The first outside funding round is expected to close sometime between the end of this year and early next year, with further rounds of fundraising planned to continue over the following three to four years as the broader buildout progresses. The company's chief executive has said the pace of investment will be adjusted according to actual demand rather than committing to the full build all at once, with South Korean officials framing the project as targeting not just domestic AI demand but also computing needs from elsewhere in the region, including Japan and Taiwan. The sheer scale of the figures being discussed underscores just how enormous the capital requirements for AI infrastructure have become even for well-established telecommunications companies, let alone smaller firms trying to compete in the same space.

Turning to national news, new analysis of federal government annual reports shows Australia's public service paid out roughly one hundred million dollars in redundancy and separation payments across just three departments over the past financial year, as a wider wave of voluntary redundancies continues to sweep through the Commonwealth public sector. The Department of Home Affairs accounted for the bulk of that spending, paying out close to seventy five million dollars in redundancies, nearly ten times what it spent the year before, with the department saying the program was designed to help it reach what it called a more financially sustainable staffing position. Figures show close to forty per cent of the Home Affairs staff who took redundancy packages were at the executive level, prompting some commentary about whether the cuts are being targeted appropriately. More broadly, the federal government spent in the order of three hundred million dollars on separations and redundancies across the entire public service over the past financial year, with more than twenty federal agencies now having announced some form of voluntary redundancy round. The Finance Minister has maintained that the public service remains roughly the right size overall, while public sector unions have criticised the scale and pace of the cuts, arguing they risk hollowing out institutional knowledge and service delivery capacity across affected departments.

In Queensland, small business groups and the state government have voiced growing anger over federal legislation that would impose a minimum thirty per cent effective tax rate at the trustee level for many discretionary trusts from July of 2028, with critics warning the change could hit family-run and regional businesses particularly hard. Industry estimates suggest somewhere in the order of three hundred and fifty thousand active Australian small businesses currently operate through discretionary trust structures, meaning any broad change to how those trusts are taxed carries wide-reaching implications well beyond larger corporate structures the policy is nominally targeting. Queensland's Premier has described small and family businesses as being under siege from a series of federal decisions, listing the trust tax changes among his specific concerns, while some federal opposition figures have argued the proposal effectively taxes small businesses twice over. The federal government has signalled it may offer a specific carve-out exempting smaller businesses operating through trusts from the higher tax rate and from costly restructuring, though that detail remains part of draft legislation rather than settled law. With a parliamentary inquiry being pushed by the Greens and little prospect of the legislation passing before late this year at the earliest, business groups say the resulting uncertainty is itself becoming a significant burden for owners trying to plan ahead.

And on the Gold Coast, police have returned two jet skis seized earlier this month after their riders allegedly operated them dangerously near a small boat carrying two young boys on the Nerang River, with authorities confirming they simply don't have the legal power to destroy the seized watercraft. The incident saw the boys' boat capsize after the jet skis came close by at speed, though neither child was injured, with the vessel's motor left damaged in the process. A teenage boy was subsequently charged over the incident, facing allegations including common assault, dangerous operation of a vessel, and wilful damage, with the matter now proceeding through the usual court process. Police confirmed the seized jet skis, recovered from addresses in Broadbeach Waters and Mermaid Waters, have now been returned to their owners simply because current laws don't give officers any mechanism to permanently dispose of seized watercraft once an investigation concludes. The case has highlighted what some water safety advocates describe as a genuine gap in the state's enforcement powers when it comes to deterring dangerous jet ski use on busy waterways like the Gold Coast's canals and rivers.
